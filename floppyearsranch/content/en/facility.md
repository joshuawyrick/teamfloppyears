## SECTION 2 — WHY FIVE ACRES MATTERS

**H2**
## Why the acreage actually matters

It sounds like a bragging number. It isn't — it's a functional one, and it changes what we can do with your dog.

**Distance is the main tool in behavior work.** A reactive dog can only learn at a distance where they're still able to think. On a sidewalk that distance doesn't exist — the other dog is twelve feet away and your dog is already gone. Out here we start at eighty yards and close it a little at a time over days. That's the entire mechanism, and you can't manufacture it in a training room.

**Dogs get separated properly.** Not every dog should meet every other dog. With this much ground, dogs who need their own space get it, instead of being rotated through the same yard on a timer.

**The work happens in real environments.** Uneven ground, wind, wildlife, machinery, other dogs at a distance, people coming and going. A recall that only works on flat turf in a quiet room isn't a recall.

**It's quiet.** Kennel stress is real and most of it is noise. Twenty runs stacked in a concrete building produce a sound environment that keeps dogs from ever settling. There's room here to keep that down.

## SECTION 3 — THE KENNELS

**H2**
## Climate-controlled indoor kennels

Indoors. Climate-controlled. Not outdoor runs, not a converted garage, not stacked crates.

That is not a luxury detail in this part of California. Corona runs triple digits for long stretches of the summer and the Santa Anas push hot dry air through here into the fall. Temperature control is a safety system, not an amenity, and it's the first thing we'd check at anyone's facility.

Each dog gets their own space with an elevated bed off the floor, their own bowls, and their own bedding — we supply it, so you don't need to send anything from home. Kennels are cleaned daily and sanitized between dogs.

**What you should ask any facility, including this one:** where do the dogs actually sleep, and can you see it. We'll walk you through the kennel building on any tour. If a facility routes your tour around the kennels, you've learned something.

## SECTION 4 — THE JAX WELLER ARENA

**H2**
## The Jax Weller Arena

Daycare here doesn't happen in a kennel. It happens in the arena — cooled turf, shade structures, multiple pools, elevated beds, and dog-safe sanitization.

The cooled turf matters more than it sounds like it should. Standard artificial turf gets hot enough to burn a dog's pads well before the air temperature looks alarming to a person. That's an avoidable injury, so we bought the equipment that avoids it.

Kennel-free means what it says: dogs are out in the space with the group and with people for the day, not cycled between a crate and a yard. The elevated beds and shade are there for dogs who want to check out for a while — but that's a dog choosing to rest, not a dog being put away.

The arena is named for Jax, the shelter dog Kyla adopted in 2012 whose behavior problems sent her into dog training in the first place. It was built as his memorial.

## SECTION 5 — THE TRAINING GROUND

**H2**
## The training ground

Open ground is where most of the actual work happens.

**Foundation work** — engagement, markers, early obedience — starts in a quiet, low-distraction area where a dog can succeed at something before we make it harder.

**Proofing** moves onto the open acreage: distance work, recall with real ground between dog and handler, heeling past distractions, place and stay while something interesting happens sixty feet away.

**Distraction exposure** is staged deliberately — other dogs at a working distance, people moving, vehicles, equipment. We raise difficulty in an order rather than hoping the dog encounters something useful.

**Off-site field trips** come last, for dogs far enough along: parks, hardware stores, sidewalks, the kind of chaos you can't build on your own land. A dog who only performs where they trained hasn't finished.

## SECTION 6 — SHADE, WATER, AND HEAT

**H2**
## Shade, water, and Inland Empire summers

Corona summers are not a minor logistical detail. They're the single biggest daily safety consideration on this property from roughly June through September.

What that means in practice:

- **Hard work moves to the ends of the day.** Training and hard play happen early and late. Midday is rest, shade, and water.
- **Water everywhere, refreshed constantly.** Pools in the arena, water in every space, checked all day.
- **Surface temperature, not air temperature.** We put a hand on the ground before a dog works on it. The cooled turf exists for exactly this reason.
- **Flat-faced and heavy-coated dogs get different rules** — shorter, cooler, more supervised days. Tell us at drop-off.
- **Indoors is climate-controlled.** There's always somewhere cool for a dog to be.

## SECTION 7 — CLEANING AND SAFETY

**H2**
## How we keep it clean and contained

**Cleaning.** Kennels are cleaned daily and sanitized between dogs. The arena uses dog-safe sanitization — the surfaces dogs live on shouldn't be treated with something you wouldn't want a dog licking, which is a lower bar than you'd hope in this industry.

**Containment.** Fencing, gates, and a leash-on rule anywhere near a vehicle or an entry point. The most common way a dog gets loose at a facility is not a fence failure — it's a car door, a gate held open a second too long, or a dog slipping a collar in a parking area. That's a procedure problem, so we run procedures.

**Introductions are staged.** New dogs aren't dropped into a group to see what happens. They're assessed first, then introduced deliberately, and dogs who don't belong in a group don't go in one.

**Vaccination requirements are enforced.** Current rabies, DHPP, and Bordetella for every dog on the property. Canine influenza recommended. No records, no admission — including for dogs we know.
