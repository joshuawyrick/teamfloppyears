## SECTION 3 — WHAT AGGRESSION ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

**H2**
## What dog aggression actually looks like

Aggression isn't one behavior with one cause. It's a category, and the category has very different members. Sorting out which one you have is most of the work.

### H3 — Dog-on-dog aggression
Your dog is fine with people and fine at home, but seeing another dog turns them into something you don't recognize. Some dogs are only like this on leash. Some only with same-sex dogs. A dog who lived peacefully with your other dog for years and suddenly doesn't is a separate problem again — household conflict between dogs who know each other gets treated differently than street trouble.

### H3 — Aggression toward people
Growling, snapping, air-snapping, or biting directed at humans. It matters enormously whether it's aimed at everyone, at strangers only, at men, at children, at people entering the house, or at people who reach for the collar. Each points at a different cause.

### H3 — Fear-based aggression
The most common kind we see, and the most misread. This dog isn't trying to dominate anyone. He's scared, and he's learned that scary things back off when he makes noise and shows teeth. The behavior looks confident. It isn't. Treating a fearful dog as though he's being defiant makes it worse in almost every case, which is why the evaluation matters so much.

### H3 — Resource guarding
Food bowls, bones, toys, the couch, a spot on the bed, sometimes a specific person. The dog goes stiff, freezes, shows the whites of the eyes, or bites when approached. Guarding is normal behavior taken past a useful threshold and it responds well to structured work. It's also the version most likely to injure a child, because kids reach for things.

### H3 — Leash aggression
On leash your dog is a problem. Off leash, in a yard, with the same dog, they're often fine. The leash removes the option to move away, sends your tension straight down the line, and stacks frustration on top. Plenty of "aggressive" dogs are precisely leash-aggressive — a better position to be in than most owners realize.

### H3 — Redirected aggression
Your dog is worked up about something he can't reach, so he bites what he can — the other dog in the house, the leash, or you. This is how a lot of owners get bitten by dogs who have never shown them a moment of aggression. It gets misread as the dog turning on the family, which is not what happened.

## SECTION 4 — AGGRESSION OR REACTIVITY?

**H2**
## Is it aggression or reactivity?

Short version: a reactive dog is overreacting to something — barking, lunging, spinning, screaming at the end of the leash — but the goal is distance or frustration, not damage. An aggressive dog intends to make contact. Plenty of dogs sit somewhere in between, and plenty of dogs labeled aggressive by an alarmed neighbor are loud, rude, and harmless.

The distinction matters because it changes the program, the timeline, and the safety plan. It also isn't something you can reliably sort out from a video or a description over the phone, which is why we look at the dog.

If your dog has never made contact with a person or another dog, start on the reactivity page — it's more likely to describe you.

## SECTION 9 — PRIVATE VS BOARD & TRAIN FOR AGGRESSION

**H2**
## Private training or Board & Train for aggression?

Both work. They work for different dogs.

**Private lessons make more sense when** the aggression is deeply fear-based and a new environment would set your dog back before it helped, when the behavior is specific to your home or your family, when you have real time weekly, or when budget is the deciding factor. Lessons are $200 a session, or $900 for five.

**Board & Train makes more sense when** the behavior is escalating, when there's a bite history and the risk at home is real right now, when you can't safely walk the dog to practice anything, or when the household can't produce daily controlled repetitions.

There's a third answer we give fairly often: start with lessons, build trust and handling skill, then move into a program if the dog is ready. Nobody is going to push you toward the more expensive option on the first call.

## SECTION 10 — SAFETY AND MANAGEMENT

**H2**
## Safety and management, starting today

Whatever you decide about training, some of this needs to happen now.

**Management is not failure.** Baby gates, crates, leashes indoors, closed doors. They're how you stop the behavior from being rehearsed, and every rehearsal makes it stronger. A dog who practices lunging at the fence forty times a week is training himself.

**Learn to use a muzzle properly.** A basket muzzle conditioned over a couple of weeks lets a dog pant, drink, and take treats, and it lets you walk your dog again without your stomach in a knot. Owners resist muzzles because of how they look. Your dog does not care how it looks.

**Stop putting your dog in the situation that's failing.** If the dog park ends badly every time, the dog park is over. If guests are a problem, your dog goes in a back room with a chew before the doorbell rings. That's not a life sentence — it's what you do while the training happens.

**Kids and dogs get no unsupervised time during this.** No exceptions, including dogs who have never shown a thing toward children.

**Get a vet involved if anything changed suddenly.** Aggression that appears out of nowhere in an adult dog is a medical question until proven otherwise. Pain, thyroid, and vision loss all produce it.

## SECTION 11 — WHAT WE CANNOT PROMISE

**H2**
## What we cannot promise

This section matters more than the program description, so read it carefully.

**We cannot guarantee an outcome.** Not on this page, not on the phone, not in your contract. Anyone who guarantees you a cured aggressive dog is either inexperienced or lying, and in this specific area of dog training that promise carries a body count. What we guarantee is honest assessment, real work, and support that doesn't expire.

**Aggression is usually managed, not erased.** The realistic goal for most cases is a dog who is safe, predictable, and livable — one who can be walked, handled, and lived with, whose triggers you understand and whose warning signs you can read. That is a genuinely different life from where you are now. It is not the same as a dog who will never again feel what he feels.

**Your dog's history doesn't get deleted.** A dog who has bitten has learned that biting works. We can teach him better options, lower his stress, and change the pattern. We cannot make the lesson unlearned, which is why the management plan is permanent and not a three-week phase.

**The result depends on you.** More here than anywhere else in dog training. Three weeks with us against fifty weeks a year at home is not a fair fight, and if the handling and structure stop at pickup the behavior comes back. We'll teach you everything we did. You have to keep doing it.

**Some dogs aren't candidates.** Occasionally we meet a dog whose aggression is too severe, too unpredictable, or too poorly matched to the household to be made safe — sometimes because there's a toddler in the house and the risk math doesn't work. When that's our read, we say it plainly and we refer you on rather than take your money. That conversation is awful and we still have it.

**We aren't veterinarians or veterinary behaviorists.** Some cases need a medical workup or medication alongside training. We'll tell you when we think that's the case and we'd rather refer than pretend.

## SECTION 14 — FAQ

**H2**
## Aggressive dog training FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**Can an aggressive dog be trained?**
In most cases, yes. Aggression has many causes — fear, resource guarding, frustration, pain, poor socialization, learned behavior — and the cause determines what training can do. Most dogs can be made significantly safer and more predictable to live with. Some cases are managed rather than resolved, and a small number of dogs aren't candidates at all. That's what the evaluation determines.

**How much does aggressive dog training cost?**
The Behavior Rehabilitation Program at Floppy Ears Ranch is $3,500 for three weeks, with a $750 deposit. Private lessons are $200 per session or $900 for five. The first 30-minute phone consultation is free, and the in-person behavior evaluation is $50, credited in full toward any program.

**My dog bit someone — what do I do?**
Make sure the person gets appropriate medical care, and write down what happened while it's fresh: what preceded the bite, where everyone was, how severe it was. In California, bites requiring medical treatment are generally reported to the county by the treating provider, so expect that animal control may contact you. Separate your dog from the situation that produced it so it can't be rehearsed. Then call a professional — don't wait for a second incident.

**Will you take a dog with a bite history?**
Often, yes. We evaluate every case individually and we need the complete, accurate history — number of bites, severity, who was bitten, and whether animal control is involved. We do turn some dogs away when we don't believe we can make the situation safe, and we'd rather tell you that than take your money.

**Is my dog aggressive or just reactive?**
Reactivity is an overreaction — barking, lunging, carrying on — usually to create distance or out of frustration. Aggression involves intent to make contact. Many dogs sit in between, and the label matters less than the evaluation. If your dog has never made contact with a person or another dog, start with our reactive dog training page.

**Can dog aggression be cured?**
Usually not in the sense of erased. The realistic outcome is a dog who is safe, predictable, and livable, whose triggers you understand and whose behavior you can manage confidently. For many dogs that looks like a normal life. It still means the management stays in place. Any trainer promising a cure is overselling.

**Do you use e-collars for aggression?**
Sometimes, depending on the dog and what's driving the behavior. It's never the first tool and never the whole plan — the foundation is relationship, engagement, and clear communication. We tell you exactly what we intend to use and why before you book, and we teach you to use it properly before your dog goes home.

**How long does aggression rehabilitation take?**
The Behavior Rehabilitation Program is three weeks of daily work, usually enough to produce substantial change. The management and handling continue for the rest of your dog's life. Anyone describing aggression as a three-week fix with nothing after it is not telling you the truth.

**Is my dog dangerous to my kids?**
We can't answer that from a website and you should be suspicious of anyone who tries. What we can say is that a dog who has shown aggression gets no unsupervised time with children while you sort this out, that resource guarding is the version most likely to injure a child because kids reach for things, and that this is exactly what the in-person evaluation exists to answer specifically.

**What if training doesn't work?**
We'll tell you if we think it isn't working, during the program rather than at pickup. Sometimes the answer is a different approach, sometimes a veterinary workup or a referral to a veterinary behaviorist, and occasionally a hard conversation about whether this dog can safely live in this specific home. Our support doesn't expire when a program ends.

**Do you work with dogs other trainers have turned down?**
Frequently. Many facilities won't take a dog with a bite history at all. We'll look at the dog before deciding, which is more than most places will do. If your landlord, insurer, or animal control is involved, say so on the first call — those cases carry deadlines we'd rather build around than discover mid-program.
