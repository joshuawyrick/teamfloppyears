## SECTION 4 — WHAT WE MEAN BY REACTIVE AND AGGRESSIVE

**H2**
## What we mean by reactive and aggressive

These words get used interchangeably and they shouldn't be.

**Reactive** usually means a dog who over-responds to a trigger — barking, lunging, screaming at the end of a leash at another dog or a stranger. Usually rooted in fear or frustration rather than intent to do damage. **Aggressive** means behavior meant to create distance through force — a dog who has bitten, snapped with contact, or made a serious attempt.

For boarding, the useful question isn't which label fits. It's what your dog does, to whom, under what conditions, and how bad it has gotten.

## SECTION 5 — THE ASSESSMENT

**H2**
## What the assessment looks at

Before a first stay we assess your dog in person. About an hour.

**What we ask you:** what the behavior is · what sets it off — dogs, men, children, strangers, handling, food, being crated · bite history, including how many and how severe · whether your dog is muzzle-conditioned · what handling is like · whether your dog can be kenneled without panicking · medical history, because pain drives a lot of this

**What we look at:** how your dog handles arriving somewhere new · response to unfamiliar people at distance and up close · response to another dog at a distance we control · leash handling · whether your dog can settle in a kennel · recovery, meaning how fast they come back down

**Be accurate with us.** Understating a bite history is the worst thing you can do here. We plan around what you tell us, and a surprise on day two is a danger to our people and to your dog. Nobody here is going to be shocked.

- Dogs with a bite history toward other dogs, not people, where the context is understood
- Dogs fearful of strangers who handle their own people fine
- Dogs who guard food, toys, or space, where management is straightforward
- Dogs kicked out of daycare for being too much rather than too dangerous

## SECTION 7 — WHAT WE MAY DECLINE

**H2**
## Dogs we may decline

This is the part most facilities leave off their website.

- Dogs with a serious bite history toward people, particularly multiple incidents or bites requiring medical treatment
- Dogs who can't be safely handled by an unfamiliar person at all — no leash on, no kennel in, no way out in an emergency
- Dogs unpredictable enough that no management plan is reliable
- Dogs whose owner's account doesn't match what we see in the assessment
- Dogs under a legal restriction, quarantine, or court order we can't accommodate
- Dogs with a possible medical driver not yet worked up by a vet

If we decline, you'll get a real reason and a real suggestion — a vet workup, a behavior program, an in-home sitter. What you won't get is a vague "we're full."

Sometimes we say yes with conditions: a muzzle for handling, no exposure to other dogs at any point, particular staff only, or a short trial stay before you book a two-week trip. Conditions aren't hedging. They're how a stay stays safe.

## SECTION 8 — HOW THESE DOGS ARE HOUSED AND HANDLED

**H2**
## How these dogs are housed and handled

**Housing.** Climate-controlled indoor kennels, placement chosen for your dog. A dog who can't handle seeing other dogs isn't housed where they'll see other dogs all day. That sounds obvious. It is not standard.

**No group play. Ever.** Not under any circumstances. Turnout is solo, on five acres, with one handler.

**Movement is planned.** Kennel to yard and back happens on a route and at a time that keeps your dog from meeting anyone. Most incidents in boarding facilities happen in hallways and doorways, not in yards.

**Consistent handlers.** Fewer people, more familiarity, fewer surprises.

**Muzzles when they help.** If your dog is already conditioned to a basket muzzle, send it — safer, and often calmer, because the handler is calmer too.

**Structure, not just containment.** Your dog still gets out, still gets worked a little, still has a routine. Warehousing a dog for a week and handing back something wound tighter isn't what we're doing.

## SECTION 11 — FAQ

**H2**
## Reactive and aggressive dog boarding FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**Do you board aggressive dogs?**
Sometimes. We assess every reactive or aggressive dog individually before a first stay, and acceptance depends on the behavior, its triggers, how severe the history is, and whether we can house and handle your dog safely. This is not a promise to accept every dog. When we decline, we say so directly and tell you why.

**How much does boarding a reactive or aggressive dog cost?**
Behavior boarding at Floppy Ears Ranch is $100 per night. Standard boarding is $80 per night. The difference covers solo turnout, planned movement through the facility, and consistent handlers, all of which take more staff time than boarding a social dog.

**Will my dog be around other dogs?**
No. Reactive and aggressive dogs get solo turnout on our five acres, never group play, and their movement through the facility is planned so they don't cross paths with other dogs. Kennel placement is chosen with that in mind too.

**Will you take a dog with a bite history?**
Sometimes, depending on the history. Bites toward other dogs, single incidents, and cases where the context is clearly understood are more often workable. Serious or repeated bites toward people, and dogs who can't be safely handled by an unfamiliar person, are frequently declined. Tell us the accurate version and we'll give you the accurate answer. If we say no, you'll get a real reason and a real suggestion — a vet workup, a behavior program, or an in-home sitter.

**Does my dog need a muzzle?**
Not always. If your dog is already conditioned to a basket muzzle, send it — safer, and often calmer. If your dog isn't conditioned to one, don't buy a muzzle and strap it on the day of drop-off. That makes things worse. We'll cover it in the assessment.

**Can boarding fix my dog's aggression?**
No. Boarding is management — safe, competent care while you're away. Changing the behavior takes a training program. Our three-week Behavior Rehabilitation Program is $3,500, and like boarding, every dog is evaluated individually before we accept the case.

**What if my dog is reactive but has never bitten anyone?**
That's the most common case we see and often the most workable. A dog who barks and lunges at other dogs but is fine with people usually just needs to be housed and moved where they aren't seeing other dogs. Still assessed individually, but the odds are good.
