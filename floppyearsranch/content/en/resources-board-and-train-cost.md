# How Much Does Board & Train Cost in Southern California?

**Direct answer** *(styled `--t-lead`)*

Board & Train in Southern California generally runs $2,400 to $8,000, depending on program length and what your dog needs. A standard three-week obedience program typically falls between $2,900 and $5,500; aggression and behavior rehabilitation programs run higher, usually $3,500 to $7,500; off-leash reliability programs run $4,000 to $8,000. Southern California prices sit meaningfully above the national average, mostly because of what land and labor cost here. At Floppy Ears Ranch in Corona, three weeks is $2,900, the six-week puppy program is $3,200, behavior rehabilitation is $3,500, and off-leash reliability is $4,500.

## SECTION 3 — WHAT DRIVES THE PRICE

**H2**
## What actually drives the price of Board & Train?

Six things, in roughly the order they matter: how long your dog stays, how many dogs each trainer handles, what problem you're solving, what equipment is included, what happens after your dog goes home, and where the facility is. Program length is the biggest single lever, because you're mostly buying a person's time. Behavior cases cost more because they take more handling, more caution, and more experienced people.

**Length.** Three weeks costs more than two for the obvious reason. Six-week puppy programs cost more than three-week adult programs even though the daily work is lighter, because six weeks is six weeks of housing, feeding, and care.

**Trainer-to-dog ratio.** A facility running eight dogs per trainer can charge less than one running three. Your dog's session count is the difference. This is the single most important number nobody publishes, and you should ask for it.

**What you're solving.** Basic obedience is a known quantity. Aggression, bite histories, and severe reactivity require slower handling, more risk management, and the most experienced person on staff. That premium is legitimate.

**Equipment.** Off-leash programs cost more partly because a quality e-collar is a real expense. Ask whether it's included or billed separately — a $4,000 program plus $350 of equipment is a $4,350 program.

**What happens after.** A program that ends at pickup is cheaper to run than one that includes a real transfer session, refresher lessons, and ongoing support. It's also worth less.

**Geography.** Five acres in Riverside County costs what it costs. A facility in coastal Orange County is paying more than that, and it shows up in the quote.

## SECTION 5 — WHY SOCAL COSTS MORE

**H2**
## Why is Board & Train more expensive in Southern California?

Southern California board-and-train pricing commonly runs roughly 20 to 30 percent above national averages. The reasons are unglamorous: commercial property and acreage cost more here, California labor costs more here, insurance costs more here, and running climate-controlled kennels through an Inland Empire summer is a real utility line. None of that is a premium for expertise — it's a premium for operating in this state.

There's a second factor that's harder to see. Land is the constraint. A trainer in Ohio can lease five acres for a fraction of what it costs in Riverside County, which is why so many Southern California programs run out of small commercial suites and rely on public spaces for real-world work. Facilities that do own ground price accordingly.

What this means practically: a $1,600 three-week program from somewhere in the Midwest is not evidence that you're being overcharged locally. Compare quotes inside your own market.

## SECTION 6 — THE CHEAP-PROGRAM RED FLAG

**H2**
## Why is a sub-$800 two-week Board & Train a red flag?

Because the math doesn't work. Two weeks is fourteen nights of housing, feeding, cleaning, and supervision before anyone has trained anything. At $800, that's under $60 a day to board your dog, train your dog, and pay staff — which means one of those three is not really happening.

Here's what's usually behind the number:

- **It's boarding with a little training attached.** Your dog gets housed and handled, plus a few short sessions. That's a legitimate service. It just isn't Board & Train, and it won't fix reactivity.
- **The dog-to-trainer ratio is very high.** Twelve dogs, one person. Your dog's actual training time is a few minutes a day.
- **There's no transfer session.** You get the dog back with a printed sheet and a handshake. Two weeks later you're back where you started and you've concluded that board and train doesn't work.
- **Corners are being cut on care.** Crated most of the day, minimal turnout, thin supervision. You won't see this on a tour that was scheduled a week out.

**The honest version of the argument:** we're not saying expensive equals good. Plenty of $6,000 programs are overpriced marketing. We're saying that below a certain number, the service being described cannot be the service being delivered, and in this market that number is somewhere well north of $800 for two weeks.

If your budget is genuinely in that range, don't buy a cheap board-and-train. Buy private lessons and do the work yourself. You'll get more for the money.

## How much does a dog trainer cost per hour?

Private, in-home or on-site dog training sessions in Southern California typically run $100 to $250 per hour, with most established trainers in the $125 to $200 range. Package pricing is standard and usually brings the per-session cost down 10 to 20 percent. Group classes are much cheaper — commonly $150 to $400 for a multi-week course — but they're a different product, because you're sharing the instructor with six other dogs.

At Floppy Ears Ranch, a private lesson is **$200**, and a five-session package is **$900** — $180 a session. A one-hour virtual consultation is **$139**, which works for people outside our driving radius or anyone who wants a professional opinion before committing to anything.

## SECTION 8 — AGGRESSION TRAINING COST

**H2**
## How much does aggressive dog training cost?

Aggression and serious behavior work in Southern California generally runs $3,500 to $7,500 for a residential program, or $150 to $300 per hour for private behavior consultations. Veterinary behaviorists — board-certified DACVB specialists, who are medical doctors, not trainers — charge more, often $300 to $600 for an initial consultation, and are the right call when medication may be part of the picture. Our three-week Behavior Rehabilitation Program is **$3,500**, with a $750 deposit.

**Why it costs more than obedience.** Fewer dogs per handler, more experienced staff, slower progress, and a genuine risk profile. A behavior case that goes wrong hurts somebody.

**What you should not pay for:** a guarantee. Nobody can guarantee an aggression outcome, and anybody who offers one is either inexperienced or telling you what you want to hear. We evaluate every aggression case individually and we decline dogs we don't think we can help safely.

## SECTION 11 — WHAT YOU'RE ACTUALLY BUYING

**H2**
## What are you actually paying for?

Concentrated time from people who do this all day, in an environment they control. That's it. There is no proprietary technique that justifies a price gap — the difference between a $2,900 program and a $5,500 program is almost always repetitions, ratio, and what happens after your dog goes home.

Which is why the comparison to make isn't price against price. It's this:

- **How many training sessions a day, and how long is each one?**
- **How many dogs is one trainer responsible for?**
- **Is the transfer session included, and how long is it?**
- **What does follow-up cost after the program ends?**
- **Who is actually handling my dog — the person selling me this, or someone else?**

A program that's $600 cheaper and includes no follow-up is not cheaper. Refresher lessons at $150 each add up fast, and the dogs that need them are the ones that needed the program in the first place.

**The uncomfortable part:** you can spend $5,000 and get nothing lasting if you don't do your half at home. Board & Train buys you a trained dog and a head start. Keeping it is on you, which is why the handoff matters more than the price tag.
