## SECTION 2 — WHAT OBEDIENCE TRAINING COVERS

**H2**
## What obedience training covers

Obedience is a shared vocabulary. Your dog learns what a handful of words mean, and that they mean the same thing every time, from every person in the house, in every room and every parking lot. It sounds unglamorous. It's also what decides whether living with your dog is easy or exhausting.

### H3 — Sit, down, stay
The three everybody knows and most dogs half-know. What separates a party trick from real obedience is duration and distraction: a sit that holds while the doorbell rings, a stay that doesn't quietly become a crawl toward you.

### H3 — Place
Go to a spot — a cot, a bed, a rug — and stay until released. The most useful command most owners have never taught, because it's an off switch. Guests arrive, dinner's on the table, a delivery driver's at the door, and your dog has a job that isn't in the middle of it.

### H3 — Heel and loose-leash walking
Two different things. Loose-leash means no tension on the line, with your dog free to sniff and look around. Heel means a specific position at your side, held. Most owners want loose-leash for daily life and heel for a parking lot. We teach both.

### H3 — Recall
Come when called. Every time, not when your dog has weighed the offer and found it competitive. It's the command that keeps a dog alive, and the one most often poisoned at home — if "come" has ever meant bath or the end of the fun, your dog has learned exactly what it means.

### H3 — Leave it
Disengage before it's in the mouth. Chicken bone on a sidewalk, a dropped pill, another dog who wants nothing to do with yours. A safety command people file under manners.

### H3 — Door manners
No bolting. Your dog waits at thresholds — front door, car door, crate, gate — until released. An open front door is one of the most common ways a dog ends up in the street.

### H3 — Impulse control
The connective tissue under all of it. Waiting to eat, staying off the counter, not jumping on a guest. A skill dogs build, not a temperament they either have or don't.

## SECTION 3 — WHY OBEDIENCE FIXES MORE THAN YOU'D EXPECT

**H2**
## Why obedience fixes more than you'd expect

Most people come to us with a behavior problem, not an obedience wish list. The dog counter-surfs, drags them down the street, jumps on grandma. Nobody wakes up thinking what my life needs is a better down-stay. But a lot of those problems dissolve under decent obedience.

**A dog with a job stops inventing one.** Most nuisance behavior is a dog with energy, no direction, and a history of getting what they want by trying things. Give them a clear instruction and a clear reward and the freelancing stops.

**It gives you something to say besides "no."** "No" tells a dog to stop. It doesn't tell them what to do instead, so they cycle through options until something works. "Place" ends the conversation.

**It changes who's making decisions.** A dog who's learned that paying attention to you pays off checks in before acting instead of after. That's most of what people mean when they say a dog listens.

Obedience is not the answer to everything, though. Serious aggression, real fear, separation anxiety, and severe reactivity need work aimed at the actual problem — obedience is a tool inside that work, not a substitute for it.

## SECTION 4 — HOW WE TEACH IT

**H2**
## How we teach it — marker training

Relationship first, then marker training, then clear corrections. That's the method, and we say it out loud before you book anything.

**Engagement comes first.** Before your dog learns a single command, they learn that paying attention to a person is worth doing. Skip this and every command afterward is a negotiation.

**A marker is a signal that means "that was right."** A word or a click, short and consistent, landing the instant your dog makes the correct choice, followed by the reward. Without one, a dog gets a treat three seconds later and has to work out which of four things earned it.

**Then clear corrections.** Once a dog reliably knows what a word means, we tell them when they're wrong as clearly as when they're right. Dogs aren't confused by rules. They're confused by rules that change depending on who's in the room.

**Then proofing.** A command that works in a quiet yard isn't trained yet. We add distance, duration, and distraction.

**On tools:** we use an e-collar in the Off-Leash Reliability Program, where the equipment is included in the price, and sometimes in other programs depending on the dog. We'll tell you exactly what we plan to use and why before you commit. You'll never find out mid-program that we're using a tool you weren't expecting.

**How much does obedience training cost in Corona, CA?**
Obedience through a three-week Board & Train is $2,900, and the six-week puppy program is $3,200. Private lessons are $200 per session or $900 for a package of five. A virtual consultation is $139. The first 30-minute phone consultation is free.

**How long does obedience training take?**
Board & Train is three weeks for dogs six months and older and six weeks for puppies. With private lessons, most owners see meaningful change in five to twelve weeks, depending on how consistently the practice happens between sessions.

**What commands will my dog learn?**
Sit, down, stay, place, heel, loose-leash walking, come when called, leave it, off, and waiting at doorways. Alongside those, the living-with-you material: settling on a place cot, crate comfort, impulse control, polite greetings, and being handled for grooming and vet visits.

**What age should obedience training start?**
Puppies can start immediately with age-appropriate work — engagement, name recognition, handling, crate and potty routines, and leash introduction. Formal obedience builds from there. There's no upper limit either: the idea that an older dog can't learn is folklore. A habit practiced for six years just takes longer to replace than one practiced for six weeks.

**Will my dog listen to me, or just to the trainer?**
That's what the transfer session is for. Your dog learns a new way of communicating and you need to learn it too, so before your dog goes home we put the leash in your hand and coach you through every command until it works for you — not until we've demonstrated it. Then free refreshers for as long as you need them.

**Do you use e-collars for obedience?**
Sometimes, depending on the dog, and always in the Off-Leash Reliability Program where the equipment is included in the $4,500. We'll tell you exactly what we plan to use and why before you book anything, and we teach you to use it properly before your dog goes home.

**Will obedience training fix my dog's behavior problems?**
Often more than people expect. Jumping, counter surfing, door dashing, pulling, and not settling usually improve substantially, because most of them are a dog with energy and no direction. Serious aggression, real fear, and severe reactivity need work aimed at the underlying behavior — obedience is part of that, not a replacement for it.
