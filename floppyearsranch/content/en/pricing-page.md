## SECTION 8 — WHY WE PUBLISH OUR PRICES

**H2**
## Why we publish our prices

Roughly half the trainers in this area make you call to find out what they charge. We've never understood the logic.

The theory is that a phone call lets someone sell you on value before you see a number. What it actually does is force everyone who's price-shopping — which is everyone — to spend a call with a stranger learning something a table could have told them in four seconds.

You should be able to work out whether this is realistic for you at eleven at night, on your phone, without talking to anybody. If it isn't, we've saved you a call. If it is, you show up already knowing the number and we spend that half hour on your dog instead of on a reveal.

Second reason: hidden pricing correlates with pricing that moves. A published number is one we're accountable to.

## SECTION 9 — IS THIS EXPENSIVE?

**H2**
## Is this expensive?

Compared to a national average, yes. Compared to what training actually costs in Southern California, $2,900 for a three-week Board & Train sits in the normal range — the low-to-middle part of it.

Here's the honest context, including the parts that don't flatter us.

### H3 — Southern California runs 20 to 30 percent above national averages

That's not a markup, it's arithmetic. Commercial land near the 91/15 interchange, the labor market for people who can safely handle a dog with a bite history, insurance, and cooling a facility through a Corona summer all cost what they cost here. A facility quoting a national-average price in this region is either subsidizing it somewhere or cutting something you'd want to know about.

For reference: three-week Board & Train programs in Southern California generally run $2,900 to $5,500. Puppy programs $3,200 to $7,500. Behavior and aggression $3,500 to $7,500. Off-leash $4,000 to $8,000. We're at the bottom of every one of those ranges.

### H3 — A sub-$800 two-week program is a red flag

Do the math. Two weeks is fourteen nights of housing, feeding, cleaning, and staffing, plus daily training and whatever handoff exists at the end. At $800 that's under $60 a day. Our boarding alone — no training at all — is $80 a night.

Something has to give, and it's usually one of these: the dog is kenneled most of the day and trained for fifteen minutes, the staff-to-dog ratio is far higher than anyone will tell you, there's no transfer session, or the low number is a door-opener and the real cost arrives as equipment and "required" follow-ups at pickup.

Cheapest is a negative signal in this market. Informed buyers read a sub-$800 two-week program as incomplete or unsafe, and they're usually right.

### H3 — What you're actually buying at $2,900

Three weeks of daily work from people who do this professionally, on five acres, in climate-controlled housing. But what decides whether the money was worth it comes at the end and after:

- **The transfer session.** Your dog learns a new way of communicating and you don't. Without a real handoff you've bought three weeks of rented behavior that evaporates in a month. This is where board-and-train earns its bad reputation, and it's the first thing to ask about anywhere you're comparing.
- **Free refresher lessons, indefinitely.** Standards drift, life gets busy. A refresher catches it before it's the original problem again.
- **Lifetime support.** Text us in three years. No expiration, no upsell attached.
- **All equipment included**, e-collar included in the off-leash program.

Divide $2,900 across the years you'll have this dog and it's a different-looking number. That's not a sales line, it's how the math works on a one-time cost against a ten-year relationship.

### H3 — And the honest part

Board & Train isn't the cheapest way to get a trained dog. Private lessons at $200 a session are, if you have the time and you'll actually do the homework. Five sessions is $900 and plenty of people get where they're going on that.

Board & Train costs more because you're buying back your own time. If your schedule can produce the repetitions, tell us on the consultation and we'll point you at the cheaper option. A client who books the wrong program is a bad outcome for both of us.
