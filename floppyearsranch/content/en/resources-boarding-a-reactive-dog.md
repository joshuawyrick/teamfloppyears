## SECTION 1 — H1 + DIRECT ANSWER

**H1**
# Can You Board a Reactive Dog?

**Direct answer** *(styled `--t-lead`)*

Yes, but not just anywhere. Most boarding facilities are built around group play and screen out any dog who fails a social temperament test, which rules out a large share of leash-reactive and dog-selective dogs. What you need is a facility that boards dogs individually rather than in groups, staffs people who read dog body language for a living, and assesses your dog before the first stay instead of finding out at drop-off. Those exist — there just aren't many, and they book up.

## SECTION 2 — WHY YOU KEEP GETTING TURNED DOWN

**H2**
## Why do boarding facilities turn away reactive dogs?

Because the standard boarding model is built on group play, and group play requires dogs who can be safely mixed. A facility running forty dogs in two play yards with three attendants is staffed for social dogs. Yours makes the math dangerous, and they know it.

That's a defensible business decision, even when it's infuriating on your end. The trouble is what it leaves you with.

You have a wedding, a work trip, a hospital stay, or a funeral. The dog you love screams at other dogs on leash and has never actually hurt anything. The place down the street says no, the second place says no after the temperament test, and a neighbor isn't an option. People cancel trips over this. Some people lie on the intake form, which ends badly for everyone including the dog.

**You're not the first person to make this call and your dog is not unusual.** Leash reactivity is one of the most common behavior problems in pet dogs. It's just poorly served by an industry organized around daycare.

## SECTION 3 — WHAT MAKES A FACILITY ABLE TO DO IT

**H2**
## What makes a boarding facility able to handle a reactive dog?

Individual housing, staggered movement, and staff who can read a dog before anything happens. The physical setup matters — separate kennels, solid barriers, controlled transitions — but the real variable is whether the people on the floor recognize a dog going over threshold thirty seconds before it happens.

What you're looking for:

- **Individual kennel housing, not group housing.** Your dog gets a space and doesn't share it.
- **Staggered turnout.** Dogs come out one at a time or in fixed, known pairings. Nobody's dog crosses paths in a hallway by accident.
- **Solid barriers where it matters.** Chain link between two reactive dogs is a fight waiting for a trigger. Visual barriers reduce the rehearsal.
- **Handlers who train dogs, not just clean kennels.** A trainer-run facility is genuinely different here. Everyone on the floor reads body language all day.
- **A real assessment before the first stay.** Not a five-minute meet-and-greet. Someone should watch your dog handle arrival, handling, a leash, and a novel environment.
- **A willingness to say no.** A facility that accepts every dog isn't screening, which means the dog in the next kennel wasn't screened either.

**What doesn't matter as much as the website suggests:** acreage, luxury suites, webcams, and whether there's a TV in the kennel. None of that keeps a reactive dog safe.

## SECTION 4 — WHAT TO DISCLOSE

**H2**
## What should I tell the boarding facility about my dog?

All of it, in specific terms, before the assessment. Understating your dog's behavior is the single most common way this goes wrong — the plan gets built off what you said, and a surprise in the first twenty-four hours ends the stay and sometimes hurts somebody.

Nobody is going to be shocked. Places that handle these dogs have heard everything.

**Tell them:**

- What exactly your dog reacts to — dogs, men, kids, bikes, hats, the vacuum
- At what distance it starts, and how it escalates
- Whether there has **ever** been contact with a person or another dog, including a nip that didn't break skin
- Whether your dog guards food, toys, beds, or people
- How your dog handles being handled — collar grabs, nail trims, being lifted
- How your dog does at the vet and the groomer
- Whether your dog has been kenneled before, and how that went
- Whether your dog has ever escaped a crate, a fence, or a collar
- Any medication, including behavior medication, and the dosing schedule
- What works. Every owner knows things about their dog that no assessment surfaces in an hour

**On behavior medication specifically:** there's no stigma. Fluoxetine, trazodone, gabapentin — a lot of dogs are on something, and a facility that judges you for it isn't the right facility. Just tell them the schedule and send the bottle labeled.

## SECTION 5 — QUESTIONS TO ASK

**H2**
## What should I ask before booking?

Ask the operational questions, not the philosophical ones. You want to know how your dog physically moves through the day and who is responsible for them at each point. Vague, warm answers to specific questions are the thing to notice.

1. **Are dogs housed individually or in groups?**
2. **Will my dog ever be in a space with an unfamiliar dog?**
3. **How do dogs get from the kennel to the yard — do they ever pass each other?**
4. **What's between my dog and the next one? Solid wall, chain link, gap?**
5. **Who handles my dog, and are they trained to handle a reactive one?**
6. **How much time out of the kennel does my dog get, and doing what?**
7. **Is anyone on the property overnight?**
8. **What happens if my dog is too stressed to eat or settle? Do you call me?**
9. **What's the emergency vet protocol, and who authorizes treatment?**
10. **What happens if you decide mid-stay that you can't manage my dog?**

That last one is the important one. A facility that has thought about it has an answer — call the owner, call the emergency contact, isolate and manage until pickup. A facility that hasn't thought about it hasn't handled many of these dogs.

## SECTION 7 — WHEN BOARDING ISN'T THE RIGHT ANSWER

**H2**
## When should you not board a reactive dog?

When your dog's reactivity is severe enough that a kennel environment is genuinely distressing rather than just unfamiliar, or when a bite risk can't be managed by a facility staffed the way that facility is staffed. Some dogs are better off with an in-home sitter, a veterinary boarding setup, or a trusted person who already knows them.

Be honest about these:

**Dogs who fall apart in confinement.** Some dogs have a real problem with kenneling — not first-night noise, but sustained panic, self-injury, refusal to eat for days. A facility can manage a stressed dog. It can't manage a dog who's dismantling a kennel door.

**Dogs on complex medical management.** If your dog needs medication timed to the hour or has a condition that could turn quickly, veterinary boarding is worth the extra money.

**Dogs with a serious bite history.** This is a different category from reactivity, and it needs a facility set up specifically for it — plus complete disclosure, and an acceptance that some places will say no for good reasons.

**And a real alternative worth considering:** if you were already thinking about training, some people combine the trip with a Board & Train. Your dog is staying somewhere anyway. Three weeks of daily training instead of three weeks of boarding is more money, but the dog you pick up is a different dog. It only works if the timing genuinely lines up — don't force it.

## SECTION 8 — SETTING YOUR DOG UP

**H2**
## How do I set my reactive dog up for a good stay?

Do a short trial stay before the trip that matters, send your dog's own food, and don't build the drop-off into an emotional event. A dog who has already spent one night somewhere handles the five-night stay far better than a dog whose first experience is the week you're in another state.

- **Do a one-night trial run** a few weeks ahead. This is the single most useful thing on this list.
- **Send their own food**, pre-portioned, plus a couple of extra days' worth. Changing diet and environment at the same time produces a week of diarrhea.
- **Send medication in the original labeled container** with written instructions.
- **Keep drop-off short and boring.** Long goodbyes read as "something is wrong."
- **Skip the sentimental blanket.** Most facilities launder everything anyway, and some dogs guard a familiar item in an unfamiliar place.
- **Make sure vaccinations are current well ahead of time** — rabies, DHPP, and Bordetella are standard requirements, and Bordetella is the one that quietly lapses.
- **Leave a real emergency contact** who can actually make a decision if you're unreachable.

## SECTION 9 — FAQ

**H2**
## Boarding a reactive dog FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**Can you board a reactive dog?**
Yes, at a facility that houses dogs individually rather than in group play, staggers turnout so dogs don't cross paths, and assesses your dog before the first stay. Standard daycare-model kennels usually can't, which is why reactive dogs get turned away so often. Expect to pay a premium and to book further ahead.

**Will a boarding facility take my dog if she barks and lunges at other dogs?**
Many won't, but leash reactivity alone doesn't disqualify a dog at a facility set up for individual handling. What matters far more is whether there has ever been contact with a person or another dog, and whether your dog can be safely moved around the property. Disclose everything honestly at the assessment — understating it is what causes a stay to end badly.

**How much does boarding a reactive dog cost?**
Expect roughly 20 to 40 percent above standard boarding, because the handling is individual instead of grouped. Standard boarding in the Corona and Inland Empire area generally runs $45 to $95 a night. At Floppy Ears Ranch, standard boarding is $80 a night and behavior or reactive dog boarding is $100 a night, with an assessment required first.

**What should I tell a boarding facility about my reactive dog?**
Everything: what triggers the reaction, at what distance, whether there has ever been any contact with a person or another dog, whether your dog guards food or resources, how they handle being handled, any escape history, and any medication including behavior medication. Nobody who works with these dogs will be shocked, and the safety plan gets built off what you tell them.

**Do reactive dogs get stressed at boarding?**
Some settle within a day or two; some find the environment genuinely difficult. A one-night trial stay a few weeks before a longer trip is the best way to find out which yours is without discovering it while you're out of state. Ask the facility to call you if your dog isn't eating or settling by day two.

**What if my dog has bitten someone — can she still be boarded?**
That's a different category from reactivity and it requires a facility specifically set up for it, complete disclosure of the bite history, and an individual assessment. Some facilities will say no, and for genuinely dangerous dogs that's the right answer. We assess these dogs case by case in Corona and we don't accept all of them.
