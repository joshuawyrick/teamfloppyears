## SECTION 3 — WHAT IS A REACTIVE DOG?

**H2**
## What is a reactive dog?

**Direct answer:** A reactive dog responds to a specific trigger with an outsized emotional reaction — barking, lunging, whining, spinning, hackles up, pulling hard toward or away — that they can't easily come back from. The reaction is driven by fear, frustration, or overexcitement rather than an intent to do harm. Most reactive dogs are entirely normal in every other setting.

The mechanical piece is threshold. Every reactive dog has a distance at which they can see a trigger and still think. Past that line they can't hear you, can't take food, and can't respond to a command they know perfectly well in the kitchen. Owners usually interpret that as stubbornness. It isn't. A dog over threshold has genuinely stopped processing.

Almost all of reactivity training comes down to finding that distance, working just inside it, and moving it closer over time. That's it. It's not complicated. It is, however, close to impossible to arrange on a sidewalk where the triggers show up whenever they feel like it.

## SECTION 4 — REACTIVE VS AGGRESSIVE

**H2**
## Reactive vs. aggressive — what's the difference?

**Direct answer:** Reactivity is an overreaction aimed at creating distance or venting frustration. Aggression involves intent to make contact and do damage. A lunging, barking dog who has never touched another dog in four years of blowing up is reactive. A dog who has bitten is in different territory. Many dogs sit somewhere in between, and unchecked reactivity can develop into aggression over time.

The reason this matters is that the two get treated differently, and mislabeling costs you either way. Owners of loud, harmless dogs sometimes convince themselves they have a dangerous animal and stop trying. Owners of genuinely aggressive dogs sometimes call it reactivity because it's the softer word, and skip the safety planning they needed.

If your dog has made contact with a person or another dog, read the aggression page instead.

## SECTION 5 — LEASH REACTIVITY

**H2**
## Leash reactivity

The most common version by a wide margin, and the most misread.

A leash does three things at once. It removes your dog's ability to create distance, which is what an uncomfortable dog wants most. It forces a head-on approach, which in dog language is rude — loose dogs meet in arcs, not straight lines. And it transmits every bit of your tension directly down six feet of nylon into your dog's neck, right at the moment you spot the trigger and tighten up.

Which is why plenty of dogs who scream at other dogs on leash play with those same dogs perfectly well off leash. That's a good sign, not a coincidence. It usually means the underlying social skills are intact and what's broken is the walk.

We work leash reactivity by rebuilding the walk itself — a real heel with a defined position, engagement with you as the default, and controlled exposure at a distance your dog can handle. Then we close the distance a little at a time, and only when the dog is ready.

## SECTION 6 — REACTIVITY TOWARD OTHER DOGS

**H2**
## Reactivity toward other dogs

Dog-directed reactivity has a few common origins, and the origin changes the plan.

**Frustrated greeters** are the most common and the most misunderstood. This dog loves other dogs and desperately wants to reach them. Being prevented produces a tantrum that looks identical to aggression to everyone watching, and these owners get some of the worst treatment from strangers on the street.

**Fear-based reactivity** comes from a dog who wants other dogs to go away and has learned that a loud display achieves it. From the dog's side, it works every time. That's why it sticks.

**Undersocialized dogs** don't have the vocabulary. Pandemic-era puppies, rural rescues, and dogs who spent their formative months without much exposure land here. Not scared, exactly. They just don't know what any of this means.

**Learned reactivity** is a routine repeated so often it's become the default response to seeing a dog, whatever started it. This is the version that gets worse with time, and the reason we push people not to wait.

## SECTION 7 — REACTIVITY TOWARD PEOPLE

**H2**
## Reactivity toward people

Barking and lunging at people is more socially costly for owners, and it makes normal life difficult — deliveries, guests, a walk down a busy street, a vet visit.

Sometimes it's specific: men, hats, beards, uniforms, wheelchairs, kids on scooters. Those patterns usually point at a gap in early exposure rather than a bad experience. Sometimes it's territorial and tied to the property line, the door, or the car window. Sometimes it's overexcitement in a dog who genuinely likes people and cannot cope with the sight of one.

The work is the same shape — threshold, engagement, an alternative behavior your dog can actually perform — but the setup differs, which is another reason we look at the dog before recommending a program.

## SECTION 8 — OVEREXCITEMENT VS FEAR

**H2**
## Overexcitement or fear?

They look nearly identical from the end of the leash and they need close to opposite handling, which is why this is the single most useful thing an evaluation sorts out.

**The overexcited dog** leans in. Loose, wiggly body, tail high and fast, weight forward, and the noise stops the instant they get to the thing. This dog needs impulse control, a clear structure, and to learn that the leash is not a countdown to a party.

**The fearful dog** wants out. Weight back even while barking, tail low, ears pinned, hackles up, and the noise doesn't stop when the trigger gets closer — it gets worse. This dog needs distance, confidence, and time. Push a fearful dog the way you'd push an overexcited one and you'll make the problem substantially worse.

Well-meaning owners get this backwards constantly, usually because a video or a well-known trainer described the other type. It isn't a knowledge failure. It's genuinely hard to read from inside the situation, while holding the leash, with your heart going.

## SECTION 9 — WHY IT GETS WORSE

**H2**
## Why reactivity gets worse without training

Because it works. Every single time.

Your dog barks and lunges, the other dog moves away — because you moved, or they did. From your dog's side, the display produced the result. That's a rehearsal, and it just got reinforced. Four times a week for a year is two hundred successful repetitions of the exact behavior you want gone.

Meanwhile the trigger list grows. Big dogs, then all dogs, then dogs on TV, then a collar jingling two houses down. And the walks shrink, because you're avoiding, which means less exposure and a dog even less equipped when it happens anyway.

That's not a reason to panic. It's a reason not to spend another six months hoping your dog grows out of it. Dogs don't grow out of practiced behavior. They get better at it.

## SECTION 12 — WHAT TRAINING LOOKS LIKE

**H2**
## What reactivity training actually looks like

Less dramatic than you'd expect. Most of it is quiet.

**1. Engagement.** Your dog learns that checking in with a person is worth doing, in an empty field with nothing happening. Boring, and everything else rests on it.

**2. A real heel.** Not "doesn't pull." A defined position your dog can hold, so there's something concrete to ask for when a trigger appears instead of a vague hope they'll behave.

**3. Finding the threshold.** We put a trigger out at a distance and watch. The number where your dog notices but can still think is where the work actually happens.

**4. Working the edge.** Repetitions just inside that line. Notice the trigger, look back at the handler, get paid. Over and over, ending while your dog is still winning.

**5. Closing the gap.** Distance comes down gradually, and when it goes badly we back up. Progress here is not linear, and anyone who tells you otherwise hasn't done much of it.

**6. Making it real.** New places, different dogs, moving triggers, other people holding the leash — a dog who only performs on our property hasn't learned anything you can use.

**7. Handing it to you.** The transfer session, then free refreshers and lifetime support for as long as you own the dog.

## SECTION 15 — FAQ

**H2**
## Reactive dog training FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**What is a reactive dog?**
A reactive dog overreacts to a specific trigger — most often other dogs, sometimes people, bikes, cars, or skateboards — by barking, lunging, spinning, or pulling. The reaction comes from fear, frustration, or overexcitement rather than an intent to do harm, and most reactive dogs are completely normal in every other setting.

**Is my dog reactive or aggressive?**
Reactivity is an overreaction meant to create distance or vent frustration. Aggression involves intent to make contact. A dog who has barked and lunged for years without ever touching another dog is reactive. A dog who has bitten is in different territory. If you're not sure, that's exactly what the evaluation is for.

**Can a reactive dog be fixed?**
Most reactive dogs improve substantially with structured training. Many become dogs you can walk anywhere without thinking about it. Some remain dogs who need a little more space than average, and that's still a completely normal life. What we won't tell you is that a specific dog is guaranteed a specific outcome — we'll give you a realistic read after we've seen your dog.

**How long does reactivity training take?**
Our three-week Board & Train produces significant change in most reactive dogs, followed by ongoing handling from you. Private lesson clients usually see meaningful progress in five to twelve weeks depending on how consistently they practice. Reactivity work is not linear — good weeks and bad weeks are normal and expected.

**Why does my dog only react on leash?**
The leash takes away your dog's ability to move away, forces a head-on approach that dogs find rude, and carries your own tension straight down to your dog. Many dogs who scream at other dogs on leash play fine with them off leash. That's a good sign — it usually means the social skills are intact and the walk is the problem.

**Will another dog in the house help?**
Sometimes, and sometimes it doubles the problem — two dogs feed each other's reactivity and now you're walking a duet. Getting a second dog to solve reactivity in the first one is a decision we'd want to talk through before you make it, not after.

**Should I use a prong collar or a head halter?**
Depends on the dog, the handler, and the behavior. Tools are a small part of this and no piece of equipment fixes reactivity by itself. We'll tell you what we recommend and why after we've watched your dog, then teach you to use it correctly rather than handing it over and wishing you luck.

**Can you help if my dog is reactive toward people?**
Yes. It's less common than dog-directed reactivity and the setup differs, but the underlying work is the same — threshold, engagement, and an alternative behavior your dog can actually perform under pressure.

**My dog is fine at home. Why is he like this outside?**
Home has no triggers and a predictable routine. The street has neither. A dog who is calm indoors and frantic outdoors is completely normal for this problem, and it's why nobody believes you when you describe it.

**How much does reactive dog training cost in Corona, CA?**
Board & Train is $2,900 for three weeks. Severe cases go into the $3,500 Behavior Rehabilitation Program. Private lessons are $200 per session or $900 for five. The first 30-minute phone consultation is free and the in-person evaluation is $50, credited toward any program.
