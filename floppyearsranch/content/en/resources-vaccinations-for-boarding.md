## SECTION 1 — H1 + DIRECT ANSWER

**H1**
# What Vaccinations Does My Dog Need for Boarding in California?

**Direct answer** *(styled `--t-lead`)*

Nearly every boarding facility, daycare, and board-and-train program in California requires three vaccines: rabies, DHPP (distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, parainfluenza), and Bordetella. Canine influenza is strongly recommended and is required by a growing number of facilities. Rabies is the only one required by state law — California law requires rabies vaccination for dogs over four months old. The other requirements are facility policy, and they exist because a kennel is a group environment.

*This article explains what facilities require. It isn't veterinary advice — your dog's vaccine schedule is set by your veterinarian.*

## SECTION 2 — THE REQUIREMENTS TABLE

**H2**
## Which vaccines are required for boarding?

The table below is the standard requirement set across California boarding, daycare, grooming, and training facilities. Individual facilities vary at the margins, so confirm with yours — but if you have these four current, you'll be admitted almost anywhere.

| Vaccine | Protects against | Typical requirement | How often |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rabies** | Rabies virus | **Required by California law** for dogs over 4 months | 1-year or 3-year vaccine depending on the product and your vet's protocol |
| **DHPP / DA2PP** | Distemper, adenovirus (hepatitis), parvovirus, parainfluenza | Required by nearly every facility | Typically every 1–3 years after the puppy series |
| **Bordetella** | Kennel cough (Bordetella bronchiseptica) | Required by nearly every facility | Commonly every 6–12 months for dogs in group settings |
| **Canine influenza (H3N2 / H3N8)** | Dog flu | Recommended; required by some facilities | Initial two-dose series, then annually |
| **Leptospirosis** | Leptospira bacteria | Not usually required; often recommended in California | Annually |

**Bordetella is the one that catches people out.** Many facilities want it every six months rather than annually for dogs in group environments, so a dog who was fine in March can be out of compliance in October. Check the date on the certificate, not your memory.

## SECTION 3 — RABIES AND CALIFORNIA LAW

**H2**
## Is the rabies vaccine legally required in California?

Yes. California law requires that dogs over four months of age be vaccinated against rabies, and dogs must be licensed with their local jurisdiction with proof of current rabies vaccination. This is state law and local ordinance, not a facility preference — which is why no boarding facility, groomer, or trainer in California can waive it.

Practical notes:

- The vaccine must be administered by, or under the supervision of, a licensed veterinarian to count.
- A three-year rabies vaccine is standard after the initial one-year booster, but which product your dog receives is your vet's call.
- Riverside County, like every California county, requires dog licensing with current rabies proof. If you're new to the area, this is usually the paperwork people forget.
- **There is no titer substitute for rabies.** Antibody titers are a legitimate conversation for some other vaccines. Rabies is a legal requirement with a defined interval, and a titer doesn't satisfy it.

## SECTION 4 — WHY FACILITIES REQUIRE THE REST

**H2**
## Why do boarding facilities require Bordetella and DHPP?

Because a boarding facility is a group environment, and the diseases these vaccines cover spread in exactly that setting. Parvovirus survives in the environment for months and kills puppies. Kennel cough spreads through the air and through shared surfaces the way a cold moves through a preschool. The requirement isn't about your dog's risk in isolation — it's about the dog in the next kennel.

**A vaccine requirement only works if it's universal.** This is why any facility that waives records "just this once" for a dog they know is doing something worse than being inconsistent. The dog whose owner was allowed to skip it is now standing next to a puppy who is three weeks past their last booster.

We enforce this without exception, including for dogs we've known for years and dogs who "never go anywhere."

## SECTION 5 — BORDETELLA SPECIFICS

**H2**
## How does the Bordetella vaccine work, and how long does it last?

Bordetella comes in three forms — intranasal (up the nose), oral, and injectable — and protection is generally considered to last six to twelve months. Intranasal and oral versions tend to produce protection faster, often within about 72 hours, while the injectable version requires two doses several weeks apart for a dog who has never had it. Ask your vet which form your dog got, because it changes the timing.

**Important limitation, stated plainly:** the Bordetella vaccine does not prevent all kennel cough. "Kennel cough" is a syndrome caused by several different organisms — Bordetella bronchiseptica is the most common but parainfluenza, canine adenovirus, mycoplasma, and others contribute. A vaccinated dog can still catch a cough. What the vaccine does is meaningfully reduce the likelihood and typically the severity.

Any facility that promises your dog cannot catch kennel cough is overstating it.

**If your dog develops a cough after a stay:** it's usually mild and self-limiting, but call your vet, keep your dog away from other dogs, and tell the facility. They need to know, and a facility that wants to know is a good sign.

## SECTION 6 — PUPPIES

**H2**
## When can a puppy be boarded, and what vaccines do they need?

A puppy generally needs to have completed enough of their vaccination series to be reasonably protected before entering a group environment, which usually means the DHPP series finished — commonly around 16 weeks — plus rabies, which California requires by four months. Bordetella can typically be given earlier. Your veterinarian sets the actual timeline, and it depends on when the series started.

The tension here is real and worth naming. The socialization window closes around 14 to 16 weeks, and waiting for full vaccination means waiting through the most important developmental period a dog has. The answer is not "keep the puppy home until 16 weeks" and it isn't "take the puppy everywhere at 9 weeks" either. It's controlled exposure to known, vaccinated, healthy dogs in clean environments.

## SECTION 7 — TITERS AND EXEMPTIONS

**H2**
## What if my dog can't be vaccinated, or I titer instead?

Send the documentation and have the conversation early rather than at drop-off. Antibody titers for distemper and parvovirus are a legitimate approach that some veterinarians support, and many facilities — including ours — will look at titer results. Rabies is the exception: it's a legal requirement with a defined interval and a titer does not satisfy it.

**If your dog has a documented medical exemption** signed by a veterinarian, bring it. Some dogs genuinely cannot receive certain vaccines. That's a conversation, and the answer depends on the specific vaccine, the specific dog, and what else is on the property that week.

**What isn't going to work:** deciding on your own that your dog doesn't need one. A facility that accepts that from you is accepting it from everyone.

## SECTION 8 — RECORDS AND TIMING

**H2**
## How do I send my vaccination records, and how far ahead?

Get vaccines updated at least two weeks before your dog's stay, and send the records ahead rather than bringing them on the day. Most vaccines need time to produce protection, and a booster given the morning of drop-off isn't doing the job it's supposed to do.

**What counts as a record.** A certificate or printout from your veterinarian showing your dog's name, the vaccine given, the date administered, the due date, and the clinic. A clear phone photo is fine if the dates are legible. A handwritten note is not, and neither is a receipt.

**How to send it.** Email it ahead of time, or have your vet's office send it directly — most clinics will do this with a phone call. Bring a copy at drop-off as a backup.

**Two weeks ahead, minimum.** If your dog has never had Bordetella and gets the injectable version, you need considerably longer — that's a two-dose series. Call your vet as soon as you book, not the week of.

## SECTION 10 — FAQ

**H2**
## Boarding vaccination FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**What vaccinations does my dog need for boarding in California?**
Rabies, DHPP (distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, parainfluenza), and Bordetella are required by nearly every California boarding facility. Canine influenza is strongly recommended and required by some. Rabies is the only one mandated by state law — California requires rabies vaccination for dogs over four months old.

**Is the rabies vaccine required by law in California?**
Yes. California law requires rabies vaccination for dogs over four months of age, and dogs must be licensed with their local jurisdiction with proof of current vaccination. No boarding facility, groomer, or trainer in California can waive it, and an antibody titer does not satisfy the legal requirement.

**How long before boarding does my dog need to be vaccinated?**
Plan on at least two weeks before the stay so the vaccines have time to produce protection. If your dog has never had Bordetella and receives the injectable form, allow longer — that version requires two doses several weeks apart. Call your veterinarian as soon as you book.

**How often does my dog need the Bordetella vaccine for boarding?**
Protection is generally considered to last six to twelve months, and many facilities require it every six months for dogs in group environments. It's the requirement that lapses most often, so check the date on the certificate rather than relying on memory.

**Does the Bordetella vaccine prevent kennel cough?**
Not entirely. Kennel cough is a syndrome caused by several organisms, and the vaccine targets the most common one — so a vaccinated dog can still catch a cough, usually milder. Any facility that promises your dog cannot catch kennel cough is overstating what the vaccine does.

**Can my puppy be boarded before finishing their vaccine series?**
Generally a puppy needs the DHPP series completed, commonly around 16 weeks, plus rabies, which California requires by four months. Your veterinarian sets the actual timeline based on when the series started. For our six-week Puppy Board & Train we work out the timing with you on the free consultation.

**What if I titer instead of vaccinating?**
Send the titer results and we'll talk — titers for distemper and parvovirus are a legitimate approach that many facilities will consider. Rabies is the exception, because it's a legal requirement with a defined interval that a titer doesn't satisfy.

**Do you require canine influenza vaccination?**
We recommend it but don't require it. It's worth having for any dog spending time in group environments, and a growing number of California facilities are moving it to required — so if your dog boards regularly, ask your vet about it at the next visit.
