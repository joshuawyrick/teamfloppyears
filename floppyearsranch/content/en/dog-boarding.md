## SECTION 2 — WHY BOARD AT A TRAINING FACILITY?

**H2**
## Why board at a training facility?

Because the person walking past your dog's kennel notices things.

Everyone here reads dog body language all day — it's the actual job. A dog stiffening up, a dog who's stopped eating, a dog guarding a bowl, play tipping from fun into too much: those get caught in the first hour, not on day three when it becomes an incident. Most boarding facilities are staffed by people who love dogs and mean well. Loving dogs and reading dogs are different skills, and only one of them prevents problems.

The second difference: nobody here hands your dog back with new bad habits. Dogs learn constantly, whether or not anyone intends to teach them, and a week of barking at a kennel door for attention is a week of practice at barking for attention. We run structure — doors, thresholds, waiting to eat, calm before out — so the week your dog spends here doesn't undo the work you've done at home.

You're not paying for a spa. You're paying for competence.

## SECTION 4 — EXERCISE AND OUTDOOR TIME

**H2**
## Exercise and outdoor time

Five acres, and your dog gets out on it.

Boarding dogs get multiple turnouts a day — potty first thing and last thing, plus time on the property in between. Some get structured group play. Some get a solo run. Some get a leash walk and a decompression sniff around the perimeter, which for a certain kind of dog beats an hour of chasing a ball.

We don't run dogs into the ground. There's a version of boarding where every dog is exhausted at pickup and the owner reads that as good value. It isn't. A dog who's over-aroused for a week comes home wired, not relaxed. Rest is in the schedule on purpose.

In the summer, hard exercise moves to the cooler ends of the day. We don't take requests on that.

## SECTION 5 — FEEDING

**H2**
## Feeding

Bring your dog's own food. Portion it out if you can — labeled bags, or a container with a scoop and written instructions.

Changing a dog's diet and their environment in the same week is how you get three days of diarrhea and a dog who won't eat, so we don't do it. If your dog is on something specific — raw, prescription, home-cooked — send it and tell us how it's handled.

The feeding schedule matches home. Twice a day, once a day, slow feeder, separate room, hand-fed because they're picky. Write it down and we'll follow it.

Some dogs eat less the first day or two in a new place. That's normal and we watch it. If your dog isn't eating by day two, you'll hear from us.

## SECTION 7 — CLIMATE CONTROL

## Climate control

The kennel building is climate-controlled and indoors, year-round.

Inland Empire summers are the real risk to a boarded dog, and heat is unforgiving with the dogs it hits hardest — bulldogs, pugs, boxers, anything flat-faced, plus seniors, heavy coats, and dogs carrying extra weight. ## SECTION 8 — HOW DOGS ARE SOCIALIZED

**H2**
## How dogs are socialized

Group play is earned and supervised, not automatic.

New dogs meet the group slowly, in a controlled space, with a person watching the whole time. We match on play style, size, and energy rather than putting every dog in one yard and hoping it sorts itself out. A hard-charging young lab and a nine-year-old terrier are not a play group, no matter how friendly both of them are.

When play gets too fast or one dog stops enjoying it, we break it up before it escalates. That's the part that requires actually reading dogs, and it's what separates a facility where nothing happens from one where you get a phone call about a scuffle.

## SECTION 9 — DOGS WHO WOULD RATHER NOT SOCIALIZE

**H2**
## Dogs who would rather not socialize

Plenty of dogs don't want to play with strangers, and that's not a defect.

Some are selective. Some are old. Some are anxious in groups. Some are perfectly friendly and simply don't enjoy other dogs, the same way plenty of people don't enjoy parties. Those dogs get solo turnout, one-on-one time with a person, structured leash walks, and quiet. They don't get pushed into a play group so we can say your dog socialized.

Tell us honestly what your dog is like. Nobody here is judging you, and the accurate version gets your dog a better week than the flattering one.

## SECTION 10 — REACTIVE AND AGGRESSIVE DOGS

**H2**
## Reactive and aggressive dogs

We assess these dogs individually and we board some of them. We don't board all of them.

If your dog has been turned away elsewhere, has a bite history, or can't be safely walked past another dog, we're one of the few places in the area that will even have the conversation — because our people handle dogs like this daily in the training programs. It's the normal job, not a special accommodation.

But being willing to look at a case isn't the same as accepting it. Acceptance depends on the assessment: what the behavior is, what triggers it, how severe it's been, and whether we can house and handle your dog safely. Sometimes the answer is no, and when it is you'll get it straight rather than a runaround.

Behavior boarding is $100 per night and requires an assessment before your first stay.

Every dog boarding here needs current **rabies**, **DHPP**, and **Bordetella**. **Canine influenza** is recommended but not required.

Bring records at drop-off or have your vet email them ahead. Bordetella is the one people most often let lapse without realizing — most vets want it every six to twelve months for dogs in group settings, so check the date rather than assuming.

**How much does dog boarding cost in Corona, CA?**
Standard boarding at Floppy Ears Ranch is $80 per night. Holiday boarding is $100 per night, and boarding for reactive or aggressive dogs is $100 per night with an assessment required first. Additional dogs from the same household are $10 less per night each. Feeding, medication, and turnout are included.

**What vaccinations does my dog need for boarding?**
Current rabies, DHPP, and Bordetella are required. Canine influenza is recommended but not required. Bring records at drop-off or have your vet send them ahead.

**Do you crate dogs overnight?**
Dogs sleep in individual climate-controlled indoor kennels, not stacked crates and not outdoor runs. Dogs from the same household can share if they normally sleep together, at $10 off the nightly rate for each additional dog. Daytime daycare is kennel-free in the Jax Weller Arena.

**Can you give my dog medication?**
Yes, at no additional charge. Send it in the original container with written instructions covering dose, timing, and whether it goes with food. For diabetic dogs, seizure medication, or post-surgical care, call us before you book so we can talk it through.

**Do you board aggressive or reactive dogs?**
Sometimes. We assess every reactive or aggressive dog individually before accepting a stay, because acceptance depends on what the behavior is and whether we can house and handle your dog safely. Our staff work with these dogs daily in the training programs, so we'll at least look at a case that other facilities decline. If the answer is no, we'll tell you plainly.

Your dog's food for the stay, medications in their original containers, vaccination records, and your vet's contact information. Leave toys, beds, and blankets at home — we provide bedding, bowls, leashes, and collars.

Weeks, not days. Thanksgiving and the December 20 through January 2 window fill first, followed by summer holiday weekends. Booking early costs nothing and there's no fixing it once the dates are gone.

**Can I see photos of my dog while I'm away?**
Yes. You get photo and video updates during the stay, and you can text us whenever you want an answer about how your dog is doing. You're not bothering anyone.

New clients need one before the first stay. It's short — we meet your dog, see how they handle the space and the people, and confirm boarding is the right fit.
