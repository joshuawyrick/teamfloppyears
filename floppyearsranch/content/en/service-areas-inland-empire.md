## SECTION 2 — WHAT THE REGION HAS IN COMMON

**H2**
## What Inland Empire dogs have in common

The IE isn't one market — it's two counties and several million people — but the dog problems rhyme, because the conditions do.

Most of the housing built here in the last twenty-five years is tract housing on compact lots. Big house, small yard, and a dog that gets fifteen minutes of actual movement a day. A lot of the workforce commutes, or works warehouse shifts on a schedule that doesn't line up with an evening walk. Summer runs long and hot enough that from June to September the only realistic exercise window is before 8am, which nobody uses.

Add it up and you get an under-exercised, under-structured adolescent dog with too much energy and no job. That describes a large share of the dogs we see from this region, and it's a solvable problem — but not by adding another walk.

The other regional constant is the heat. Every part of what we do accounts for it: indoor climate-controlled kennels, cooled turf in the daycare arena, shade structures, pools, and training sessions scheduled around the afternoon rather than through it.

## SECTION 4 — CITY BY CITY

**H2**
## The cities we serve

### H3 — Jurupa Valley
The closest of these, 15 to 25 minutes depending on whether you're in Mira Loma, Glen Avon, Pedley, or Rubidoux. Take Limonite or Van Buren to the 15 south. Jurupa Valley is the one city on this page where daycare is genuinely practical.

It's also the least uniform. There are tract neighborhoods that look like Eastvale and there are large-lot rural-residential properties with horses, and the training problems split accordingly — small-yard adolescent chaos on one side, no-recall dogs on acreage on the other. Everything we offer is available here, including boarding at $80 a night.

### H3 — Chino
20 to 25 minutes up the 71, which begins at the 91 in Corona and runs straight into Chino Valley. Chino still has working agricultural land alongside the newer master-planned neighborhoods on the south side, which makes for a wide mix of dogs — ranch dogs, family dogs, and a lot of first dogs in brand-new houses.

Board & Train, private lessons, behavior work, and boarding all work from Chino. If you're at the south end near the Preserve, daycare is worth asking about.

### H3 — Chino Hills
Also about 20 to 25 minutes on the 71, and one of the shorter drives on this page despite feeling farther. Chino Hills is hills, canyons, narrow winding streets, and neighborhoods that back directly onto open space and Chino Hills State Park.

That geography produces its own list: dogs that bolt on a hillside with nothing to stop them, coyote encounters and the reactivity that follows one, and yards where a fence ends at a slope the dog can simply go around. Recall matters more here than in flat neighborhoods, which is why the Off-Leash Reliability Program at $4,500 gets asked about a lot from this side of the 71.

### H3 — Ontario
25 to 30 minutes north on the 15. Ontario has grown enormously on the south end — thousands of new homes below the 60 — and those neighborhoods have the classic profile: new house, new family, new dog, small yard, both adults commuting.

We take Board & Train, puppy programs, private lessons, and boarding from Ontario. If you're near the airport and travel for work, boarding at $80 a night with trainer supervision is worth the drive over a kennel closer in.

### H3 — Rancho Cucamonga
Roughly 30 to 35 minutes — the 15 north, then west on the 210 or up through Etiwanda depending on where you are. Alta Loma and the north-side foothill neighborhoods sit right against open space, and the Pacific Electric Trail runs the width of the city, which means a lot of dogs here get walked in public around a lot of other dogs.

That's excellent for a dog with manners and rough for one without. Leash reactivity is the most common thing we hear about from Rancho. Board & Train at $2,900 and behavior work at $3,500 both make sense at this distance; daycare does not.

### H3 — Fontana
30 to 35 minutes via the 15 north. Fontana is one of the largest cities in the region and one of the most dog-heavy, with a lot of adopted and rehomed dogs of the types that get judged on sight.

Worth saying directly: we've spent years on bully-breed rehabilitation and rescue work — more than two hundred pit bull saves — and a breed label doesn't decide whether we take your dog. Behavior does. If you've been turned away somewhere because of what your dog looks like rather than what it's done, call us.

### H3 — Upland
About 35 to 40 minutes, and the outer edge of what's on this page. From Upland we offer Board & Train and behavior work. Daycare is out and we'd steer you toward something closer for routine boarding — at that distance it stops being worth your afternoon.

Upland is San Bernardino County, and the full write-up lives on that page.

## SECTION 5 — BOARD & TRAIN

**H2**
## Board & Train from the Inland Empire

Three weeks for dogs six months and older at $2,900. Six weeks for puppies at $3,200. Three weeks at $3,500 for aggression, bite histories, and severe reactivity, with every case evaluated individually before we accept it.

Your dog trains multiple times a day, every day, in an environment we control, and then gets proofed around real distractions before going home. That's the honest reason it costs what it costs — concentrated time, from people who do this all day. It isn't a factory reset and your dog doesn't come back a different animal.

What makes it hold is the handoff. Before your dog goes home we spend real time putting the leash in your hand and coaching your timing, your body language, and how to hold a line without raising your voice. Then free refresher lessons and lifetime support, indefinitely. Any program that skips that part is selling you three weeks of rented behavior.

While your dog is here: a photo every day, video every third day, a call partway through, and you can text us whenever you want.

## SECTION 7 — FAQ

**H2**
## Inland Empire dog training FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**Do you serve the Inland Empire?**
Yes. Our facility is in Corona at the south end of the 15 and the 71, which puts most of the Inland Empire 20 to 40 minutes away — Jurupa Valley, Chino, Chino Hills, Ontario, Rancho Cucamonga, Fontana, and the near edge of Upland. Board & Train, private lessons, behavior work, and boarding are all available.

**Is it worth driving 30 minutes for Board & Train?**
For a program you drop off and pick up, yes — you make the trip twice, not daily. The comparison that matters isn't distance, it's what you get at the end of it: five acres, indoor climate-controlled kennels, and daily training, versus a training room in a shopping center.

**Do you offer dog daycare in Ontario, Rancho Cucamonga, or Fontana?**
No. Daycare runs at our Corona facility and those cities are 25 to 35 minutes away, which is an hour or more of driving a day. That isn't a service, it's a chore you'll quit. Daycare from this region only makes sense from Jurupa Valley or the south end of Chino.

**Will you take my dog if it's a pit bull or a bully breed?**
Breed doesn't decide it. Behavior does. Rescue and rehabilitation work with bully breeds is a large part of this business — more than two hundred pit bull saves — and we evaluate every dog individually regardless of what it looks like.

**How do you handle the summer heat?**
Kennels are indoors and climate-controlled, not outdoor runs. The daycare arena has cooled turf, shade structures, pools, and elevated beds. Training sessions are scheduled around the hottest part of the day rather than pushed through it. Inland summers are not something to improvise around.

**Can I board my dog in Corona if I live in Chino Hills?**
Yes. Standard boarding is $80 a night, $100 on holidays, with $10 off per additional dog from the same household. It's about 25 minutes down the 71. Reactive dogs and dogs other kennels have declined are $100 a night and require an assessment first.
