## SECTION 2 — WHEN TO START

**H2**
## When should puppy training start?

**Direct answer:** The week your puppy comes home. The critical socialization window closes somewhere around 14 to 16 weeks, and what your puppy learns about the world before then shapes how they handle it for the rest of their life. You do not wait until six months, and you do not wait for the last vaccine — you manage the risk instead, which we explain further down this page.

Here's the part people find hard to believe: your eight-week-old puppy is already learning. Not "will start learning soon." Right now. Every time the biting gets a reaction, every time the crying gets the crate door opened, every time jumping earns attention. Nobody sat down and taught those. They were learned anyway, because puppies are learning constantly and don't care whether a lesson was intentional.

That's the argument for starting early, and it's not really about obedience. A twelve-week-old puppy doesn't need a perfect heel. A twelve-week-old puppy needs to learn that people are worth paying attention to, that new things are fine, that being handled is fine, and that a crate is a normal place to be.

Everything is easier now than it will be in a year. That's the entire pitch.

## SECTION 3 — WHAT WE WORK ON

**H2**
## What we work on

### H3 — Socialization
The most misunderstood word in dog training. Socialization is not letting your puppy meet every dog and person they see — that's how you build a dog who pulls toward every stranger and melts down when they can't get there. Real socialization is controlled, positive exposure to the world: surfaces, sounds, stairs, umbrellas, vacuum cleaners, hats, kids, bikes, car rides, and calm adult dogs who won't teach your puppy anything you'll have to undo. The lesson is that new things are unremarkable.

### H3 — Potty training
Structure, supervision, and a schedule. Most potty training failures are supervision failures — the puppy was loose in a room where nobody was watching, and the accident happened out of sight, so nothing was communicated. Crate use, timed trips, and paying attention for two weeks solve almost all of it.

### H3 — Crate training
A crate that your puppy likes is one of the most useful things you'll ever build. It makes potty training work, gives your puppy an off switch, keeps them safe when you can't watch, and means that a vet stay or a groomer's kennel later in life isn't a crisis. Crying in a crate is normal for the first few nights. Opening the door in response to the crying is what turns three bad nights into three bad months.

### H3 — Biting and nipping
Puppy teeth are genuinely awful and the arms of every new puppy owner look like they lost a fight. It's normal, it's developmental, and it peaks somewhere around 12 to 16 weeks. What we teach is bite inhibition and an alternative — how hard is too hard, what to put in the mouth instead, and how to end play before it escalates. What doesn't help is holding the muzzle shut or hitting a puppy, both of which show up in a lot of online advice and both of which produce a puppy who's hand-shy.

### H3 — Leash introduction
Before a heel, before loose-leash walking, a puppy has to learn that the leash isn't a fight. Short sessions, low pressure, and a lot of movement toward things the puppy already wants. Puppies who learn early that pulling gets them nowhere don't turn into dogs who drag you down the block.

### H3 — Recall foundation
The most valuable command your dog will ever have, and the one most easily ruined. A puppy who comes running every time is not a trained puppy — a puppy who comes running every time is a puppy nobody has taught to ignore the word yet. We build recall as something that always pays and never predicts the end of the fun, on a long line, before your puppy is old enough to have opinions about it.

### H3 — Confidence building
Novel surfaces, low obstacles, mild problem-solving, and safe exposure to things that move and make noise. A confident puppy is dramatically less likely to become a reactive or fearful adolescent, and confidence is far easier to build at four months than to repair at fourteen.

### H3 — Handling
Ears, paws, nails, mouth, tail, collar grabs, being lifted, being held still. Every one of those is something a vet, a groomer, or you in an emergency will need to do, and the time to teach it is when your puppy weighs eleven pounds and thinks it's a game.

### H3 — Household manners
Not jumping on people. Not counter surfing. Not bolting through open doors. Settling down while the family eats. Being fine when left alone. These are the behaviors that decide whether living with a dog is pleasant, and they're all much easier to install than to remove.

## SECTION 6 — ADOLESCENT REGRESSION

**H2**
## The adolescent regression nobody warns you about

**Direct answer:** Somewhere between six and twelve months, most well-trained puppies appear to forget everything they learned. Recall stops working. The dog who never pulled starts pulling. New fears show up out of nowhere. This is normal adolescent development, not a training failure, and it is the single most common point at which owners give up on a dog.

Nobody tells new owners about this. Then it happens, and the story an owner tells themselves is that the training didn't work, or the dog is being spiteful, or they got a bad one.

**What's actually happening**

Your dog is a teenager. The brain is reorganizing, hormones have arrived, and the world has become far more interesting than you are. Independence goes up. Impulse control goes down — physically, temporarily down, not a character flaw. A second fear period commonly shows up in this stretch, which is why a nine-month-old who was fine with the trash cans on Tuesday is convinced they're lethal on Thursday.

**What it looks like from your side**

- Recall that worked perfectly at five months stops working at eight
- Selective hearing that seems pointed and personal
- Pulling, jumping, and counter surfing coming back after months of nothing
- Barking at things that were never an issue
- Sudden fear or suspicion of specific objects, people, or dogs
- The beginnings of leash reactivity, which is where a lot of reactive adults are born

**What to do about it**

**Don't take it personally, and don't quit.** The training is not gone. It's underneath, and it comes back if you keep asking.

**Go back to management.** Long line in the yard. Leash in the house. Fewer chances to practice ignoring you. Give freedom back once it's earned again, not on schedule.

**Stop calling your dog when you know they won't come.** Every ignored recall makes the word weaker. Get the long line back on and make the answer certain again.

**Handle the new fears carefully.** Don't flood a fearful adolescent and don't drag them up to the scary thing. Distance, patience, and letting them approach on their own terms.

**Keep the structure.** This is the stage where households quietly stop enforcing the rules because everyone is tired. That's what turns a rough patch into a permanent set of habits.

**It ends.** Most dogs come out the other side somewhere between 12 and 18 months, larger breeds later. The dog you trained is still in there.

If you've hit this wall and you're losing ground, this is a very good moment for a refresher or a program. Adolescent regression is one of the most common reasons a dog arrives here at nine months, and it's one of the most straightforward to turn around — the foundation is already built, it just needs enforcing.

## SECTION 7 — VACCINATION AND SOCIALIZATION SAFETY

**H2**
## Vaccination and socialization safety

This is the one real tension in puppy raising: the socialization window closes before the vaccination series finishes.

Wait for the last shot at 16 weeks and you've spent the most important developmental period of your dog's life indoors. Ignore the vaccines entirely and you risk parvo, which is a real disease that really kills puppies, and which lives in soil for a long time.

The workable answer is neither extreme. Socialize deliberately, in controlled places, with known dogs.

**Reasonable during the series:** carrying your puppy through public places, car rides, friends' homes with vaccinated adult dogs, your own yard, meeting people of all kinds, clean surfaces, sounds, and handling.

**Worth avoiding until the series is done:** dog parks, pet store floors, rest stops, and any public grass where unknown dogs have been.

**Our facility requirement:** puppies need to be far enough along in their vaccination series to be safe around other dogs before they join a program. Exactly where that line falls depends on your puppy's age and schedule, so we'll walk through the timing on the free consultation. Current rabies, DHPP, and Bordetella are required, with canine influenza recommended.

**Ask your vet.** They know your puppy, your area, and what's circulating locally, and their read beats a general recommendation on a website.

## SECTION 10 — FAQ

**H2**
## Puppy training FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**What age should puppy training start?**
As soon as your puppy comes home, usually around eight weeks. The critical socialization window closes around 14 to 16 weeks, so the early weeks matter more than any other period in your dog's life. You don't need to wait for the full vaccination series — you manage the risk by choosing controlled environments instead of dog parks and pet store floors.

**How do I stop my puppy from biting?**
Puppy biting is normal, developmental, and peaks around 12 to 16 weeks. Teach bite inhibition rather than trying to eliminate mouthing outright: end play the moment teeth go too hard, redirect onto something your puppy is allowed to chew, and make sure the household is consistent about it. Don't hold the muzzle shut or use physical corrections on a young puppy — that produces a hand-shy dog and doesn't fix the biting.

**How long does potty training take?**
Most puppies are reliable within four to eight weeks of consistent work, though full bladder control develops with age and accidents happen into the fourth or fifth month. The variable isn't the puppy, it's the supervision. Crate use, a schedule, and not leaving your puppy loose and unwatched account for nearly all the difference between a fast result and a slow one.

**Is my puppy too young for Board & Train?**
Puppies six months and under go into the six-week Puppy Board & Train at $3,200. The practical limit is vaccination status — your puppy needs to be far enough along in the series to be safe around other dogs. We'll go through the timing for your specific puppy on the free consultation.

**Can my puppy be around other dogs before all their shots?**
Around known, vaccinated, healthy adult dogs in controlled settings, yes, and it's important that they are. Avoid dog parks, pet store floors, and public grass where unknown dogs have been until the series is complete. Parvo is a genuine risk and so is an undersocialized adult dog — the answer is choosing environments carefully, not staying home.

**Why did my 8-month-old puppy stop listening?**
Adolescence. Somewhere between six and twelve months most dogs go through a stage where recall stops working, old habits come back, and new fears appear. It's normal brain and hormone development, not spite and not a training failure. Go back to a long line and management, keep the structure, and stop giving commands you can't enforce. Most dogs come out of it between 12 and 18 months.
