## SECTION 3 — THE FOUNDATION: RELATIONSHIP FIRST

**H2**
## Relationship first, always

Nothing in a program starts with a correction. Not on day one, not on any dog. The first thing your dog learns here is that people are worth paying attention to — a few days of engagement work with food, play, movement, and a person more interesting than the environment. It looks unstructured. It isn't.

The reason for that order is practical, not philosophical. A correction only works as information if the dog already knows the right answer. Correcting a dog who was never taught the behavior isn't communication — it's pressure, and pressure with no way out is how you build a dog who shuts down or bites. So the sequence is fixed: **relationship, then teaching, then clarity, then proofing.**

And "relationship" doesn't mean your dog loving you. Your dog already loves you — that was never the problem. It means your dog looking to you for the answer when something happens. Barking at the door, lunging at the dog across the street, guarding the couch — a lot of that is a dog doing a job nobody else was doing.

## SECTION 4 — MARKER TRAINING

**H2**
## Marker training, explained

**Direct answer**

Marker training is the use of a consistent sound or word — a clicker, or a spoken "yes" — at the exact instant a dog does the right thing, followed by something the dog values. The marker doesn't reward the dog. It tells the dog precisely which choice earned the reward that's about to arrive.

Timing is the whole thing. Dogs live in a much narrower window than we do. If your dog sits and you reward two seconds later, you may well have paid for standing back up. The marker closes that gap to nothing.

We use three: **"yes"** (that's the behavior, payment is coming), a **continuation marker** (keep doing that), and a **release** (you're free). Then we build duration, then distance, then distraction, in that order. Skipping ahead to distraction is the most common mistake owners make, and the reason a dog who sits perfectly in the kitchen ignores you at the park.

A dog trained this way is offering behavior, not bracing for it — which is exactly what makes a correction meaningful information later rather than a scare.

## SECTION 5 — CORRECTIONS

**H2**
## Why we correct, and what that actually means

Marker training tells a dog what's right. On its own, it tells them nothing about what's wrong. For a lot of dogs that's fine — if the worst thing your dog does is fail to sit promptly, you don't need the other half of the conversation. But when a dog is lunging at a jogger, bolting through the front door toward a road, or putting teeth on a family member, "reward an alternative behavior and manage the environment forever" isn't a serious plan. It requires perfect management from imperfect people in a house with a screen door.

A correction is the second half of a language your dog already understands. It says *no, not that* — at the right moment, at the lowest level that gets through — followed immediately by a chance to do the right thing and get paid for it.

**H3**
### The rules we hold ourselves to

- **The dog knows the behavior first.** We never correct a dog for failing at something we haven't taught.
- **Lowest effective level.** If the dog notices, it's enough. If a correction has to escalate to work, the plan was wrong and we change the plan.
- **Immediate, then over.** No lingering, no nagging, no repeating it because it felt good to be right.
- **Always a way out.** Every correction is paired with a clear, achievable right answer. Pressure with no exit produces fear, and fearful dogs bite.
- **Never in anger,** and never pushed through. Nobody here raises their voice at a dog. Some dogs get worse under correction — fearful dogs, trauma cases, pain-driven behavior — and those get a different plan, not a harder one.

**What corrections are not for:** teaching, making a dog "respect" you, or standing in for exercise, structure, or a vet appointment for a dog who might be in pain. If a trainer's answer to every problem is more correction, that isn't balanced training. That's one tool and a lot of confidence.

## SECTION 6 — THE TOOLS

**H2**
## The tools we use, and when

**H3**
### Flat collar, slip lead, and long line

Most of what happens here happens on the simplest equipment there is. A well-taught dog on a flat collar and a fifteen-foot line is the majority of a Board & Train program. The long line does the early recall work — it lets a dog be wrong safely, which is how they learn to be right.

**H3**
### Prong collar

A prong collar spreads pressure evenly around the neck instead of concentrating it on the trachea the way a flat collar does when a dog hits the end of a leash. Used correctly it takes very little pressure to communicate.

**When we use one:** strong, high-drive dogs pulling hard enough to damage their own throat, and leash reactivity where the handler needs to say something immediately and quietly. **When we don't:** small dogs, fearful dogs, dogs with neck or tracheal issues, and any dog where a lighter answer is working. It also comes off — a prong is a training tool, not a lifestyle.

**H3**
### E-collar (remote collar)

An e-collar delivers a stimulation the handler controls, at a level the handler sets. Modern units have well over 100 levels, and the working level for most dogs is one they barely notice — a tap on the shoulder, not a shock.

**When we use one:** the Off-Leash Reliability Program, always, by design — the collar and all equipment are included in the $4,500. Selectively in other programs, and only after the on-leash foundation is solid.

**Why for off-leash specifically:** at two hundred yards, in a field, with a rabbit in play, you have no leash. What you have is a way to say something from a distance. Without that, "off-leash reliability" means hoping, and hoping is how dogs end up in roads.

**H3**
### Prong collar vs. e-collar — the actual difference

| | Prong collar | E-collar |
|---|---|---|
| **Range** | Leash length | Hundreds of yards |
| **Main job** | Clarity on leash — pulling, leash reactivity, heel | Communication off leash — recall, distance commands |
| **Feedback** | Physical, mechanical, immediate | Adjustable, dialed to the individual dog |
| **Learning curve for the owner** | Lower | Higher — timing and level both matter |
| **Typical stage** | Early to middle | Later, after the foundation holds |
| **Faded out?** | Yes, most dogs come off it | Often kept as a safety net for off-leash access |

Neither is a shortcut and neither trains a dog by itself. They're both ways of being clearer, faster, at a distance the leash can't cover.

## SECTION 7 — IS E-COLLAR TRAINING CRUEL?

**H2**
## Is e-collar training cruel?

**Direct answer**

Used the way we use one, no — but the question is fair, and the answer depends entirely on the level, the timing, and the foundation underneath it. A remote collar worked at a level the dog barely perceives, after the dog already knows the behavior, is a tap on the shoulder from a distance. A collar cranked high, used to punish a confused dog with no way to be right, is exactly what people are worried about. Both get called "e-collar training." What separates them:

**We find the dog's working level, and it's low.** Start at zero, go up one level at a time until the dog notices — an ear twitch, a head turn. That's the level, and it's usually far lower than owners expect.

**The dog already knows the behavior.** The collar is never how a dog learns what "come" means. It's how the dog is reminded at distance, after learning it on a long line.

**We teach you before your dog goes home.** You'll put the collar on your own arm and feel the level — most owners do this expecting to flinch and end up surprised. Then you'll practice under supervision until your timing is right, because bad timing is what makes this tool unfair.

**Fit and skin care are non-negotiable.** Correct placement and tightness, contact points rotated, collar off for hours every day. Pressure sores come from a collar left on all day, not from the stimulation.

**What we won't tell you:** that there's no debate here. There is. Thoughtful trainers argue that aversive tools risk fallout — fear, suppression, redirected aggression — when used poorly, and those failures do happen. Our answer isn't that the risk is imaginary. It's that the risk is a function of skill and honesty, and that for the problems people bring us the alternative carries its own: a dog managed forever, rehomed, or euthanized for something that could have been addressed.

**And if you'd rather not,** say so. For a lot of goals we can get where you want without one. Off-leash reliability, for most dogs, is not one of them.

## SECTION 8 — BALANCED VS. POSITIVE REINFORCEMENT

**H2**
## Balanced vs. positive reinforcement training

**Direct answer**

Positive-reinforcement-only training teaches dogs by rewarding desired behavior and managing the environment to prevent unwanted behavior, with no aversive consequences. Balanced training uses the same reward-based teaching and adds corrections so the dog gets information about wrong answers too. Both approaches train dogs successfully. The disagreement is about what to do when a dog gets it wrong in a situation you can't manage your way out of.

**The honest case for purely positive:** it's lower-risk in unskilled hands. Bad reward timing wastes a session; bad correction timing can damage a dog. For puppies and a great many normal family-dog problems, it works and it works well.

**The honest case for balanced:** management-only plans depend on humans being perfect indefinitely. Doors get left open. Kids have friends over. The 15-year-old walks the dog once. When the failure mode is a bite or a dog in traffic, "we'll manage it" is a plan with an expiration date.

**Where we land.** Balanced, and transparent about it — but we don't think the other camp is populated by fools. We've inherited dogs from purely positive trainers whose foundation made our job easy.

**How to decide:** ask any trainer what tools they might use on your dog, at what point, and what would make them stop and change approach. Answered specifically, in either camp, that's a trainer you can work with.

## SECTION 9 — IN PRACTICE, AND WHAT WE WON'T DO

**H2**
## What this looks like in a program — and what we won't do

Week one is relationship and teaching. Week two is clarity — the behaviors are known, so we hold the standard, add distraction, and bring in leash tools if they're part of your dog's plan. Week three is proofing, plus off-leash work for dogs in that program. Then the transfer session, where the leash goes in your hand and we coach your timing until it works for you rather than for us.

**Four things we won't do:**

- **Guarantee a behavioral outcome.** Especially on aggression. Anyone who does is selling something they can't deliver.
- **Use a tool we didn't tell you about.** Ever. It's in writing before you book.
- **Hurt a dog to make a point.** Anything past the lowest effective level is a trainer with a temper.
- **Take a case we don't think we can help,** or work a dog whose behavior looks medical without a vet involved. Sudden aggression, anxiety, or reactivity in an adult dog is pain or thyroid often enough to rule out first.

## SECTION 10 — FAQ

**H2**
## Training methods FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**What training methods does Floppy Ears Ranch use?**
We're a balanced training facility. Every dog starts with relationship building and marker training, which is how behaviors are taught. Once a dog understands a behavior, we add clear corrections so they get information about wrong answers too. Tools range from a flat collar and long line up to a prong collar or e-collar depending on the dog and the goal, and we tell you exactly what we plan to use before you book. Balanced does not mean punishment-based — the teaching half comes first, always.

**Do you use e-collars on every dog?**
No. The e-collar is part of the Off-Leash Reliability Program by design, and the equipment is included in that program's price. In other programs we use one selectively, and only after the on-leash foundation is solid. If you'd rather we didn't, tell us — for many goals there's another route, and we'll say honestly whether yours is one of them.

**How do you decide what level to use on an e-collar?**
We start at zero and go up one step at a time until the dog just notices — an ear flick or a head turn. That's the working level, and it's usually much lower than owners expect. You'll feel it on your own arm before your dog goes home, then practice under supervision until your timing is right. The tool becomes unfair when it's set too high, used on a dog who doesn't know the behavior, or used with bad timing.

**What's the difference between a prong collar and an e-collar?**
A prong collar works at leash distance and spreads pressure evenly around the neck instead of concentrating it on the trachea. It's mainly for clarity on leash — pulling, heeling, leash reactivity. An e-collar works at hundreds of yards and is mainly for communication off leash, especially recall. Most dogs are eventually faded off a prong collar; an e-collar is more often kept as a safety net for off-leash freedom.

**Can you train my dog without any aversive tools?**
Often, yes, depending on the dog and the goal. Basic obedience, puppy foundations, and a great many family-dog problems are reachable with markers, structure, and management. Reliable off-leash freedom and some serious behavior cases are where we'd tell you honestly that we can't get you there that way.
