## SECTION 1 — H1 + DIRECT ANSWER

**H1**
# What to Expect from Board & Train: A Complete Prep Guide

**Direct answer** *(styled `--t-lead`)*

Pack your dog's own food for the length of the stay, any medications in labeled containers, and current vaccination records — most facilities supply everything else, and toys and blankets are usually better left at home. Expect your dog to take two or three days to settle, expect the first week of updates to look less dramatic than you hoped, and expect the last day to be the most important one, because that's the handoff. The hardest part is the drive home after drop-off, and that part is on you, not the dog.

## SECTION 2 — WHAT TO PACK

**H2**
## What do I need to pack for Board & Train?

Three things: food, medication, and vaccination records. That's genuinely it at most facilities, and it's exactly it at ours — we provide leashes, collars, bedding, bowls, and all training equipment. Overpacking is the most common thing new clients do.

**Bring:**

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Food** | Your dog's normal food, for the full stay plus 2–3 extra days. Pre-portioned in labeled bags removes all guesswork. |
| **Medication** | Original labeled containers, with written dosing instructions. Include supplements. |
| **Vaccination records** | Rabies, DHPP, Bordetella. Send them ahead if you can. |
| **Written notes** | Feeding quirks, fears, what your dog does when stressed, what works. |
| **Emergency contact** | Someone who can actually make a decision if we can't reach you. |

**Leave at home:**

- **Toys.** They get destroyed, they get guarded, and a dog who guards a toy in a new environment creates a problem that didn't exist at your house.
- **The favorite blanket.** Same reason, plus it comes back different. Bedding is provided.
- **Your own leash and collar.** We use our equipment for training, and yours is one more thing to lose.
- **Treats.** We have them, and dietary changes plus environmental changes equal a bad week.

**A note on food:** don't switch your dog's diet the week before. Changing food at the same moment you change everything else is how you get seven days of diarrhea, which slows the whole program down.

## SECTION 3 — THE WEEK BEFORE

**H2**
## How should I prepare in the week before drop-off?

Get vaccinations sorted at least two weeks out, keep your dog's routine normal, and use the last few days to write down everything a stranger would need to know about your dog. Don't try to fix anything yourself in the final week — cramming training in beforehand doesn't help and often winds the dog up.

**Do:**

- **Confirm vaccines are current and send records.** Bordetella is the one that quietly lapses.
- **Refill medication** so you're not sending a bottle with four days left in it.
- **Write the notes.** Everything: eats better if someone's nearby, terrified of the vacuum, has a bad hip on the left, panics at fireworks.
- **Trim nails** if that's a fight at your house. It's a kindness to everyone.
- **Keep the routine ordinary.** Normal walks, normal feeding, normal week.

**Don't:**

- **Don't cram training.** A week of intensive drilling before drop-off does nothing except stress you both.
- **Don't switch food.**
- **Don't skip disclosure.** If your dog snaps when you take a bone, say so. It's common, nobody's judging you, and it changes how we handle them. What we can't work around is finding out the hard way.
- **Don't make it a big emotional event.** Dogs read you accurately.

## SECTION 4 — DROP-OFF DAY

**H2**
## What happens on drop-off day?

Plan for 30 to 45 minutes. You'll hand over food, medication, and records, walk through your dog's history and habits with the trainer, sign the paperwork, and then leave — reasonably quickly, without a drawn-out goodbye. Most dogs are fine within minutes of you being out of sight. The drive home is the hard part.

**`HowTo` schema on this sequence.**

**Step 1 — Arrive within the drop-off window.** Ours is 8:00am to 8:00pm. Arrive when your dog is calm rather than after a two-hour car ride and a park visit.

**Step 2 — Hand over supplies.** Food, medication with written instructions, vaccination records, notes.

**Step 3 — Walk through your dog.** The trainer will ask about history, triggers, what you've tried, what your goals actually are. Be specific and be honest — this is where the program gets shaped. If there's a bite history, this is the moment.

**Step 4 — Paperwork.** Boarding and training agreement, emergency vet authorization, emergency contact.

**Step 5 — Hand off the leash and go.** Short and matter-of-fact. Long goodbyes tell a dog something is wrong. Ninety seconds after you're gone, your dog is investigating a new place, not grieving.

**Step 6 — Expect to feel terrible on the drive home.** Genuinely. Nearly everyone does. It passes when the first photo comes through.

## SECTION 5 — THE FIRST FEW DAYS

**H2**
## What do the first few days look like for my dog?

Decompression, not training. Most facilities spend the first day or two letting a dog settle — learning the routine, the sounds, the people — before any real work starts. Expect your dog to be a little off: less appetite, restless the first night, more sleep than usual on day two. That's normal and it resolves.

**Day 1.** Arrival, a quiet introduction to the kennel, the yard, the routine. Some dogs eat, some don't. Skipping one meal on day one is settling in.

**Day 2.** The routine starts to make sense to them. First real training sessions, usually short. Most dogs sleep hard this night.

**Day 3–4.** This is where it clicks for most dogs. They know where they sleep, when they eat, who the people are, and what the sessions are. Engagement goes up noticeably.

**The rest of week one.** Foundation work — engagement, marker training, the beginnings of obedience. Not dramatic to watch.

**What to worry about, and what not to.** Not eating for the first meal or two, restlessness the first night, and a dog who comes home a little thinner are all normal. Not eating for several days, or a dog who isn't settling by day three, is something a facility should be calling you about — not something you find out at pickup. Ask what their threshold for calling you is before you book.

## SECTION 6 — WEEK BY WEEK

**H2**
## What does a three-week Board & Train look like week by week?

Roughly: week one is foundation, week two is where the obedience gets real, week three is proofing it against distraction and then handing it to you. Progress is not linear and the videos in week one will look less impressive than the ones in week three, which is exactly the right order.

**Week 1 — Foundation.** Decompression, engagement, marker training, learning to work with a person. This is the least visually impressive week and the most important one. Everything downstream sits on it.

**Week 2 — Obedience.** Sit, down, place, heel, recall, leave it, door manners. The commands start to look like commands. This is usually where the video updates start to genuinely surprise people.

**Week 3 — Proofing and handoff.** The same skills under harder conditions — around other dogs, around movement and noise, in new environments. A recall that only works in a quiet yard isn't a recall. Then the transfer session, which is the whole point.

**For a six-week puppy program,** the pace is deliberately slower: socialization, potty training, crate training, biting, leash introduction, and handling spread across six weeks rather than crammed into three. Puppies need repetition over time, not intensity.

## SECTION 7 — UPDATES

**H2**
## What updates will I get while my dog is there?

Ask for a specific cadence before you book, and hold the facility to it. Ours is a photo every day, working video every three days, and a call partway through the program — plus you can text with a question whenever you want. If your dog gets sick or something changes, you hear from us that day, not at pickup.

**A word about the videos.** They're working footage, not a highlight reel. Which means sometimes you'll see your dog get something wrong. That's on purpose — you're paying to see what's actually happening, and a facility that only sends the perfect reps is managing your feelings rather than informing you.

**And about the mid-program call.** This is where you'll hear the honest read: what's going well, what's harder than expected, and what we're adjusting. Sometimes it includes "this is going to need more work at home than we thought." Better to hear it in week two than at pickup.

## SECTION 8 — PICKUP AND THE TRANSFER SESSION

**H2**
## What happens at pickup?

The transfer session, and it's not a five-minute handoff. Your dog has learned a new way of communicating and you haven't — so we put the leash in your hand and coach you through every command, working on your timing, your body language, and how to hold a boundary without getting loud about it. Bring everyone who lives with the dog.

**What to expect:**

- **Block out real time.** This isn't a pickup, it's a lesson.
- **Everyone attends.** Partner, kids old enough to handle the dog, whoever walks them. A dog who learns that three people enforce a rule and one doesn't will work the fourth person.
- **You practice until it works for you**, not until we've demonstrated it. There's a difference and it decides whether this was worth the money.
- **You'll get a straight list of what's likely to slip.** Some things will. Knowing which ones and what to do about them is most of the battle.
- **Your dog may be a little weird with you at first.** Excited, or oddly formal, or both. It sorts itself out in a day.

**Then:** free refresher lessons with no expiration, and lifetime support. Text us in six months, text us in three years.

## SECTION 9 — THE FIRST MONTH HOME

**H2**
## What should I expect in the first month at home?

A test period in week one, then a settling in. Your dog will check whether the rules that applied at the facility apply in your house — that's not defiance, it's information gathering, and it closes fast if you hold the line calmly. The first four weeks decide whether the training holds.

**Week 1 home.** Testing. Keep the structure tight: leash in the house if that's what it takes, the same commands with the same words, the same expectations from everyone. Ten minutes of practice a day.

**Weeks 2–4.** The most important stretch. Short daily reps, in real situations. Practice the recall in the yard before you need it at the park.

**Months 2–6.** Some drift is normal. Standards slip a little — everyone's do. Book a free refresher before it becomes the original problem.

**The honest part:** we can hand you a trained dog and teach you to handle them. We can't come to your house and enforce it. Owners who put in ten minutes a day for the first month keep what they paid for. Owners who don't watch it erode, slowly, and usually blame the program.

## SECTION 10 — FAQ

**H2**
## Board & Train prep FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**What should I pack for Board & Train?**
Your dog's normal food for the length of the stay plus a couple of extra days, any medication in original labeled containers with written instructions, and current vaccination records. Most facilities including ours provide leashes, collars, bedding, bowls, and all training equipment. Leave toys and blankets at home — they get destroyed or guarded, and neither helps.

**How long does it take a dog to settle in at Board & Train?**
Most dogs settle within two to three days. Expect a slightly reduced appetite and a restless first night, followed by heavy sleep on the second day and noticeably more engagement by day three or four. A dog who still isn't settling by day three is something the facility should be calling you about.

**Will my dog be sad or think I abandoned them?**
No. Dogs live in the present far more than we do, and a dog in a residential program is busy, engaged, and getting a lot of structured attention. The separation is genuinely harder on the owner than on the dog — which is why daily photo updates exist.

**Can I visit my dog during Board & Train?**
Most trainers discourage mid-program visits, because for most dogs they reset the adjustment rather than reassure them. What matters more is the update schedule: ask for daily photos and regular working video before you book. You can text us with a question any time your dog is here.

**What happens at Board & Train pickup?**
The transfer session, where the trainer puts the leash in your hand and coaches you through every command your dog learned — timing, body language, and how to hold a boundary. Bring everyone in the household who handles the dog. Block out real time for it; a five-minute handoff at the gate is not a transfer session and it's where board-and-train programs fail.

**Should I change my dog's food before Board & Train?**
No. Changing diet at the same time you change environment is how you get a week of digestive upset, which slows the whole program down. Send the food your dog already eats, pre-portioned if you can, with a few extra days' worth.

**How much should I practice with my dog after Board & Train?**
About ten minutes a day for the first month, in real situations rather than a formal session. That's the window that decides whether the training holds. After that, consistency of the rules matters more than practice volume — and a free refresher session catches any drift before it becomes the original problem again.
