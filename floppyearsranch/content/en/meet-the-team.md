## SECTION 2 — WHY THIS PAGE EXISTS

**H2**
## Why this page exists

You're considering handing your dog to strangers for three to six weeks, or leaving them overnight in a building you've been in once. You should know exactly who's on the other side of that.

Dog training is an unlicensed profession in the United States. Anyone can print a business card. That means the only real due diligence available to you is looking at who a person is, what they've actually done, what they claim, and whether they'll let you walk the property before you pay.

So here's all of it, including the part where we tell you which credentials we hold and, just as importantly, which ones we don't.

## SECTION 3 — CHAD GABRIEL

**H2**
## Chad Gabriel — Owner & Head Trainer

**Image:** Portrait, Chad working a dog on leash on the property.
*Alt: "Chad Gabriel, owner and head trainer at Floppy Ears Ranch, working a dog on leash in Corona, California"*

Chad spent about a decade in professional baseball. He was drafted in 2004 into a St. Louis Cardinals affiliate and spent four years in the organization, then played six more seasons professionally in Brisbane, Australia. He relocated from Dallas to Norwalk, California in 2015.

He did not come to dog training through a certification program. He came to it through Kyla — watching her work, then trying it, then finding out he was good at it. From there he chased it seriously: seminars, mentorships, time under trainers who were further along than he was, and a volume of hands-on repetitions that's hard to shortcut. He has handled more than 1,000 dogs.

**What he's best at.** Obedience, and fear and aggression rehabilitation. Those two sit together more naturally than people expect — a dog who has no idea what's being asked of him is a dog with a lot to be anxious about, and a large share of what looks like aggression thins out once the dog has a clear job and a handler who is finally being consistent.

**How he works.** Quiet. Chad doesn't raise his voice at dogs and doesn't tolerate handlers who do. He's blunt with people, though. If he thinks you're the reason the dog is the way the dog is, he'll say so — kindly the first time, and plainly if it needs repeating. Most clients end up grateful for that.

**His own dog.** Rosie was a rehabilitation case before she was a family dog. He'll tell you about her if you ask, and it's worth asking, because it's the most honest picture of what this work looks like when it's yours and not a client's.

**Other things worth knowing.** He was a guest on the Dog Trainer's Podcast, season 2, episode 5. Around the ranch and on Instagram he's "Captain Chad."

This section is unusual for a dog training website. We think it's the most useful thing on the page.

**What we don't have:** no veterinary behaviorist on staff. If your dog's case needs veterinary behavior medicine, that's a referral, not something we'll quietly attempt.

**Why we bother saying this.** Dog training is unlicensed. Letters after a name are the only shorthand buyers have, which is exactly why some businesses collect them loosely. You're better served by knowing what a trainer has actually done — how many dogs, what kinds, for how long, and whether they'll let you watch — than by a string of acronyms nobody verifies.

Ask us these questions. Ask the next facility too.
