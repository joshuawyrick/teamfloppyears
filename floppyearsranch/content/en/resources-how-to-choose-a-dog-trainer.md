## SECTION 1 — H1 + DIRECT ANSWER

**H1**
# How to Choose a Dog Trainer (Including the Red Flags)

**Direct answer** *(styled `--t-lead`)*

Dog training is unregulated in the United States. There is no license, no state board, and no legal requirement to hold any credential before calling yourself a dog trainer or a behaviorist. That means the burden of vetting falls entirely on you. The four things that matter most are whether the trainer will tell you plainly what methods and tools they use, whether you can see the facility before you hand over your dog, what happens at the handoff, and whether they'll tell you when you're not a good fit for their program.

---

## SECTION 2 — THE UNLICENSED PROBLEM

**H2**
## Is dog training a licensed profession?

No. In the United States, dog training is not a licensed or regulated profession in any state. Nobody has to pass an exam, complete supervised hours, carry insurance, or demonstrate competence before taking your money and working with your dog. Someone who finished a weekend seminar last month and someone with twenty years and thousands of dogs behind them can both legally use the same job title.

The word "behaviorist" is the sharpest edge of this. In most contexts it's a protected-sounding word with no protection behind it. A **veterinary behaviorist** is a real, verifiable thing — a licensed veterinarian who completed a residency and board certification. An **applied animal behaviorist** (CAAB/ACAAB) holds a graduate degree in the field. Anyone else calling themselves a behaviorist is using a word, not a credential.

This isn't an argument that credentials are everything. Some of the best trainers working today came up through apprenticeships and have no letters after their name, and some certified trainers are mediocre. It's an argument that you cannot outsource the vetting to the title on the website.

## SECTION 3 — CERTIFICATIONS DECODED

**H2**
## What do dog trainer certifications actually mean?

They're voluntary credentials from private organizations, and they vary enormously in rigor — from a multi-hundred-hour requirement plus a proctored exam to a weekend course. Knowing which is which is most of the value. Here's what the common ones actually require.

| Credential | Issued by | What it actually means |
|---|---|---|
| **CPDT-KA** | Certification Council for Professional Dog Trainers | Certified Professional Dog Trainer – Knowledge Assessed. Requires documented hands-on training hours, a reference, and a proctored exam. The most widely recognized general trainer certification in the US. Knowledge-assessed, not skills-assessed. |
| **CPDT-KSA** | CCPDT | The step above KA. Adds a hands-on skills assessment. Much less common. |
| **CBCC-KA** | CCPDT | Certified Behavior Consultant Canine – Knowledge Assessed. Specifically for behavior problems — aggression, fear, anxiety — rather than obedience. Requires substantially more documented behavior-case hours. |
| **CDBC / IAABC** | International Association of Animal Behavior Consultants | Certified Dog Behavior Consultant. Case-study based: applicants submit written case work that's peer-reviewed, plus documented hours. Behavior-focused, not obedience-focused. |
| **IAABC-ADT** | IAABC | Accredited Dog Trainer. IAABC's training-level credential, a step below CDBC. Also case-study based. |
| **KPA CTP** | Karen Pryor Academy | Certified Training Partner. Completion of a structured six-month program with in-person workshops and video-assessed skills. Strongly positive-reinforcement oriented. |
| **CTC** | Academy for Dog Trainers | Certificate in Training and Counseling. A demanding two-year distance program with a high failure rate. Positive-reinforcement oriented. |
| **IACP CDT / CDTA** | International Association of Canine Professionals | Certified Dog Trainer and Certified Dog Trainer Advanced. Requires documented experience plus submitted video of the applicant working dogs. IACP is method-neutral and includes balanced trainers. |
| **IACP professional membership** | IACP | **Membership, not certification.** Requires professional experience, references, and adherence to a code of conduct — but it is not the same as holding the CDT. Worth knowing the difference when you read a website. |
| **NADOI** | National Association of Dog Obedience Instructors | One of the oldest US credentials. Requires years of documented instructing experience and endorsement. Instructor-focused. |
| **AKC CGC Evaluator** | American Kennel Club | Approved to administer and certify the AKC Canine Good Citizen test. It is an evaluator approval, not a training certification, and it is not difficult to obtain. Useful, but it does not indicate advanced skill. |
| **DACVB** | American College of Veterinary Behaviorists | A licensed veterinarian with a residency and board certification in behavior. The only credential on this list that can diagnose medical causes and prescribe medication. The right call for severe cases. |
| **CAAB / ACAAB** | Animal Behavior Society | Applied animal behaviorist with a doctorate or master's in the field. Academic and rare. |

**How to use this table.** A credential tells you someone invested in the profession and submitted to an outside standard. It doesn't tell you they're good with your dog, and its absence doesn't tell you they're bad. Verify any credential you're told about — every one of these organizations publishes a searchable directory, and it takes two minutes.

**And know what you're reading.** "Member of" is not "certified by." "Certified" from an in-house academy the trainer also runs is not an independent credential. Neither is disqualifying. Both are worth noticing.

## SECTION 4 — METHODS TRANSPARENCY

**H2**
## What should a trainer tell you about their methods?

Exactly which tools they use, when they use them, and what happens if your dog doesn't respond. You should never discover mid-program that a prong collar or e-collar is being used on your dog. That conversation belongs before you pay a deposit, in specific terms.

The industry runs on a spectrum. On one end, purely positive-reinforcement trainers who don't use aversive tools at all. On the other, balanced or correction-inclusive trainers who use marker training plus corrective tools including prong and remote collars. Both camps contain excellent professionals and both contain bad ones.

**You do not have to pick a camp to hire well.** You have to get a straight answer. Ask directly:

- Do you use prong collars, e-collars, or slip leads? On what kind of dog and at what point in the program?
- What do you do when a dog gets something wrong?
- What's your position on food in training?
- Can I watch you work a dog like mine before I book?

**The red flag isn't the answer. It's the dodge.** "We use a customized approach based on each dog's individual needs" is not an answer. Neither is a website that talks about philosophy for four paragraphs without naming a single piece of equipment.

**Our position, since we're asking you to demand this:** we build on relationship and marker training first, then add clear corrections so a dog understands both sides of the conversation. We use an e-collar in the Off-Leash Reliability Program, where the equipment is included in the price, and sometimes in other programs depending on the dog. We say which tools we plan to use before you book anything.

## SECTION 5 — THE FACILITY TOUR

**H2**
## Can you tour the facility before you book?

If the answer is no, stop. This is the single biggest red flag in board-and-train, and it's not close. You are proposing to leave a family member somewhere for three weeks. A facility that won't show you where your dog will sleep is telling you something about where your dog will sleep.

What matters is not just whether tours are allowed, but when.

**Good signs:** you can come by during business hours without a two-week lead time. Tours happen while dogs are actually there. You get to see the kennels, not just the training field and the lobby. Nobody is steering you away from a particular building.

**Bad signs:** tours only by appointment, weeks out. Tours only on a specific day. Tours that skip the sleeping area. "We don't allow visitors for the safety of the dogs" — which is real in narrow circumstances and is also the most common cover story in the industry.

**Ask to see:** where dogs sleep, where they're fed, where they spend the hours between sessions, the outdoor area, and the cleaning setup. Look at the floors and smell the air. You'll know.

**If a program is in-home or mobile with no facility,** that's a legitimate model — but then ask where your dog will actually live during a board-and-train, how many dogs are in the home, and whether you can see it. Same question, different building.

## SECTION 6 — WHERE DOGS SLEEP

**H2**
## Where will my dog actually sleep?

Ask for specifics and expect specifics. "In our kennels" is not enough. You want to know the size of the space, whether it's indoors, whether it's climate controlled, what your dog sleeps on, and how many hours a day they're in it.

The honest range in this industry is wide. Some facilities crate dogs for eighteen to twenty hours a day. Some have indoor-outdoor runs. Some have climate-controlled indoor kennel buildings. Some house dogs in the trainer's home. All of these can be fine, and all of these can be terrible, and the only way to know is to look.

**In Southern California, climate control is not a luxury item.** Inland Empire summers run well past 100 degrees. Any facility relying on shade and airflow alone through July and August is running a risk with your dog.

**Ask:** How many hours a day is my dog crated or kenneled? What's the flooring? Is there heat and air conditioning? What happens in a power outage?

## SECTION 7 — STAFF RATIO AND OVERNIGHT

**H2**
## How many dogs per trainer, and who's there overnight?

These are the two questions that separate a serious operation from a busy one, and almost nobody publishes the answers. Ask both, and ask for numbers rather than adjectives. A facility running twelve dogs per trainer cannot be giving your dog the session count they described on the sales call.

**Trainer-to-dog ratio.** This is the number that determines how much real training your dog gets. Ask how many dogs are in the board-and-train program at one time, and how many trainers are working them. Then do the arithmetic on how much individual attention that leaves.

**Overnight coverage.** This is where the industry is vaguest, and it deserves a direct question: **is someone physically on the property overnight?** A lot of facilities say "24/7 supervision" and mean cameras. Cameras are not supervision — nobody is watching a monitor at 3am, and a camera cannot break up a fight or notice bloat.

There are honest answers here that aren't 24-hour staffing. "Dogs are secured individually overnight and someone lives on the property" is a fine answer. "Staff is on site from 6am to 9pm and dogs are separated and secured in individual kennels overnight" is a fine answer. What's not fine is a slogan.

**Ask:** Is anyone on the property overnight? Are dogs housed individually at night? Who responds if something happens at 2am, and how long does it take them to get here?

## SECTION 8 — UPDATES AND HANDOFF

**H2**
## What updates should you get, and what happens at pickup?

Ask for a specific cadence before you book — daily photos and regular working video is the reasonable standard for a multi-week program — and ask exactly how long the handoff session is. The handoff is where board-and-train succeeds or fails, and a twenty-minute demonstration at pickup is not a handoff.

**Updates.** Three weeks of silence is unacceptable and unfortunately common. What you want is a stated schedule you can hold them to: how often, what format, and whether you can text with a question. Also ask what happens if your dog gets sick or something goes wrong — you should hear about it that day, not at pickup.

**The handoff, which matters more.** Your dog will have learned a new way of communicating. You haven't. If nobody teaches you the timing, the leash mechanics, and the body language, the training will decay in a matter of weeks and you will conclude that board-and-train doesn't work.

**Ask:**
- How long is the transfer session, and is it one session or several?
- Does everyone in my household need to attend? (Correct answer: yes.)
- What does a follow-up lesson cost after the program ends?
- Is there support after the program, and does it expire?
- What are the odds something slips, and what do I do about it?

A trainer who says "some things will slip, here's which ones and here's what to do" is being straight with you. A trainer who says your dog will come home perfect is not.

## SECTION 9 — HEALTH AND SAFETY

**H2**
## What health and safety questions should you ask?

Four: what vaccinations are required and whether they're enforced, what the emergency veterinary protocol is, whether the business carries insurance, and how they handle medication. The first one is also a screening test — a facility with loose vaccination requirements is a facility that will accept your dog next to a dog with an unknown health status.

**Vaccinations.** The normal requirement set is rabies, DHPP, and Bordetella, with canine influenza recommended. Rabies is legally required in California for dogs over four months old. If a facility waves off records for a dog they know, they'll wave them off for the dog next to yours.

**Emergency vet protocol.** Ask which clinic they use, how far it is, whether there's a 24-hour emergency hospital in range, and who authorizes treatment if they can't reach you. Also ask what the financial arrangement is — you're generally responsible for the bill, and you want to know that before an emergency rather than during one.

**Insurance.** Ask whether the business carries general liability and care-custody-and-control coverage, and ask to see a certificate. Care, custody, and control is the specific coverage that applies to a dog in someone else's keeping, and plenty of otherwise legitimate operations don't carry it. Note that insurance is not licensing — no state requires it for dog trainers. This is a question you have to ask, and "we're licensed, bonded, and insured" on a website is a phrase, not a document.

**Medication.** Ask who administers it, whether there's a log, and what happens with a dog on twice-daily medication or something time-sensitive like insulin.

## SECTION 10 — MONEY TERMS

**H2**
## What should you ask about deposits, cancellations, and refunds?

Get the terms in writing before you pay anything: how much the deposit is, whether it's refundable, how much notice you need to give to reschedule, and what happens if the program is cut short. This is the paperwork nobody reads and it's where most disputes in this industry come from.

**Reasonable in this market:** a deposit of several hundred dollars to hold dates, non-refundable inside some window, transferable to a new date with reasonable notice. Balance due at or near drop-off.

**Ask specifically:**
- Is the deposit refundable? Under what conditions?
- What if I need to reschedule — how much notice, and is there a fee?
- What if you decide mid-program that my dog isn't a fit — do I get a prorated refund?
- What if my dog gets sick and has to come home early?
- Is there any guarantee, and what exactly does it cover?

**On guarantees.** Be skeptical of any guaranteed outcome, especially on aggression. Behavior isn't a product with a warranty, and the guarantees that exist in this industry are usually written narrowly enough to be unenforceable. A guarantee of *support* — free refreshers, ongoing access — is a real and honest promise. A guarantee that your dog will never bite anyone is not.

## SECTION 11 — REVIEWS

**H2**
## How much should you trust the reviews?

Read them for patterns rather than scores. A perfect five-star average across a hundred reviews with no detail is less informative than a 4.6 with specific, dated, detailed accounts. The reviews worth the most are the ones written months after the program ended and the ones describing how a problem was handled.

**What to look for:**

- Reviews that describe the dog, the problem, and what changed. Vague praise is cheap.
- Follow-up reviews. "It's been a year and she's still solid" is the review that matters.
- How the business responds to criticism. A defensive reply to a bad review tells you how a hard conversation with you will go.
- Complaints clustered around one theme. One person who felt ignored is noise. Five people who all say communication went quiet is a pattern.

**And ask for references.** Specifically: can I talk to a client whose dog had a problem like mine? A trainer with a real client base can produce one. This is more useful than any star rating and almost nobody asks.

## SECTION 12 — RED FLAGS

**H2**
## What are the red flags when choosing a dog trainer?

The disqualifying ones are refusal of a tour, refusal to name their tools, and any guarantee on a behavior outcome. The rest below aren't automatic disqualifiers, but two or three together are a pattern.

**Stop the conversation:**

- **Won't let you see the facility.** Nothing else on this list matters as much.
- **Won't tell you what equipment they use.** Either camp is fine. Evasion isn't.
- **Guarantees a behavior outcome**, especially on aggression or a bite history.
- **Claims a credential you can't verify** in the issuing organization's directory.
- **Takes an aggression case sight unseen**, over the phone, with a credit card.

**Slow down and ask more:**

- Pressure to decide today. Discounts that expire this afternoon.
- No written contract, or a contract you can't read before paying.
- Won't discuss what happens if it doesn't work.
- Dismisses your veterinarian, or discourages you from involving one.
- Talks about "dominance" and "alpha" as the whole explanation for every behavior.
- Blames you for the dog's problems as an opening move.
- No clear answer on who is actually handling your dog day to day. The person who sold you the program is often not the person doing the work — that's normal, but you should know who it is.
- Every dog gets the same program and the same price regardless of what's wrong.
- Vague on where your dog sleeps or how long they're kenneled.

17. How many training sessions a day, and how long is each?
18. What updates will I get, and how often?
19. How long is the transfer session, and who needs to attend?

**Money**
21. What's the total price, and what's not included?

## SECTION 15 — FAQ

**H2**
## Choosing a dog trainer FAQ

**`FAQPage` schema. Must match rendered text exactly.**

**Is dog training a licensed profession in the United States?**
No. Dog training is unregulated in every US state — there is no license, no state board, and no legal requirement to hold any credential before calling yourself a dog trainer or a behaviorist. All certifications are voluntary and issued by private organizations. That means vetting a trainer is entirely on the buyer.

**What certifications should a dog trainer have?**
The most widely recognized general certifications are CPDT-KA from the CCPDT and IACP CDT from the International Association of Canine Professionals. For behavior problems specifically, look for CBCC-KA or an IAABC CDBC. For severe cases involving possible medical causes, a veterinary behaviorist (DACVB) is the highest credential available. Verify any credential in the issuing organization's public directory — it takes about two minutes.

**What is the biggest red flag when choosing a dog trainer?**
Refusing to let you tour the facility before you book. You're leaving your dog somewhere for weeks, and a business that won't show you where your dog will sleep is telling you something about where your dog will sleep. Close behind it: refusing to name the specific tools they use, and guaranteeing a behavior outcome.

**What questions should I ask a board and train facility?**
Ask how many dogs are in the program at once and how many trainers, where your dog sleeps and for how many hours a day, whether anyone is on the property overnight, what specific equipment is used, how long the transfer session is and who must attend, what follow-up costs, what the vaccination requirements and emergency vet protocol are, and whether the deposit is refundable. Then ask when their program is the wrong choice for a dog — the answer tells you the most.

**Does a certification mean a trainer is good?**
It means they invested in the profession and submitted to an outside standard, which is worth something. It doesn't mean they're good with your specific dog, and its absence doesn't mean a trainer is bad — some excellent trainers came up entirely through apprenticeship. Weigh it alongside methods transparency, facility access, references from clients with similar dogs, and what happens after the program.

**Should I trust a trainer who guarantees results?**
Be skeptical, particularly on aggression. Behavior isn't a product with a warranty, and outcome guarantees in this industry are usually written narrowly enough to be unenforceable. A guarantee of ongoing support — free refreshers, no expiration — is a real promise a business can actually keep.

**Is a balanced trainer or a positive-reinforcement trainer better?**
Both camps contain excellent professionals and both contain bad ones, and the choice between them is genuinely yours to make. What matters is that the trainer tells you plainly which one they are and exactly what tools they use before you pay a deposit. Discovering an e-collar on your dog mid-program is a failure of disclosure, not of method.

**How do I verify a dog trainer's credentials?**
Every legitimate certifying organization — CCPDT, IAABC, IACP, Karen Pryor Academy, NADOI, the Academy for Dog Trainers, the American College of Veterinary Behaviorists — publishes a searchable directory of current credential holders. Search the trainer's name. Also note the difference between "member of" an organization and "certified by" it; they are not the same claim.
